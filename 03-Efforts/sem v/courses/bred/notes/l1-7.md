## 1. What is Behavioral Research?

Behavioral research is an **umbrella term** for research in the social and behavioral sciences — health policy, social work, cognitive science, HCI, management, etc. — that deals with human behavior.

Three ways to define it (know all three, they overlap):
1. **A way to examine and understand** individual and social behavior through *measurement and interpretation*.
2. **The study of observable behavior and inferred mental processes.** Goal: discover how people perceive their world, think, feel, change over time, learn, make decisions, and interact with others. Includes perception, decision-making, memory, emotion, social behavior.
3. Uses **both quantitative and qualitative methods** to measure and understand human behavior.

**Interdisciplinary relevance:** Psychology, Education, Marketing, Public Health, Neuroscience, and more.

Key working principle: researchers **collect data and systematically compare treatment and control conditions** on human behavior/choice — though a control condition is *not always possible* (e.g., you can't randomly assign people to be sleep-deprived long-term, or to have a personality trait).

**Adaptive nature:** behavioral research can work with whatever database/prior research exists — written notes, recorded interviews, or "big data."

### Why does this matter? (Skills gained)
From the textbook (Ch.1), the course builds two kinds of skill:
- **Skills as a researcher** — how to design, run, and analyze a study yourself.
- **Skills as a consumer of research** — how to critically evaluate claims you encounter (e.g., a magazine survey, a news headline "Study shows X causes Y"). Key critical questions: *Who* was studied? *How* were they sampled? *How many*? What fraction responded? Could the answers to these change the result?

### Goals of Science (textbook, Ch.3 — underpins "why do behavioral research")
Most scientists pursue three goals, and they build on each other:
1. **Understanding (description → explanation):** first just accurately *describe* what's happening (the "what"); as the description gets more complete — more factors/conditions identified — it becomes an *explanation* (the "why").
2. **Prediction:** if we understand something, we should be able to predict when/whether it will occur. Prediction doesn't always require full understanding, though (e.g., we can predict that some depressed patients improve after a treatment without fully knowing the underlying mechanism).
3. **Control:** only possible when the researcher can actually manipulate the phenomenon (this is why *experiments* — which manipulate variables — are considered the strongest design for establishing cause-effect, vs. purely observational sciences like astronomy, which can predict but never control).

### Two assumptions all scientists make (Ch.3)
1. **Determinism:** behavior is lawful/orderly — it's a function of prior (antecedent) events, not random or capricious.
2. **Discoverability:** this lawfulness can, at least in principle, be discovered through research.
- These are treated as *useful working assumptions*, not proven facts — science "sticks by the rules of the game" as long as they keep producing understanding/prediction/control, and revises them (a "paradigm shift," e.g., Ptolemy's earth-centered model being replaced by Copernicus's sun-centered one) when they stop being useful.

### Basic vs. Applied Research (Ch.1/Ch.3 — relevant to how project topics were framed in class)
- **Basic research:** aimed at understanding a phenomenon for its own sake, with no immediate practical goal in mind (not "mission-oriented").
- **Applied research:** aimed at solving a specific, practical, real-world problem.
- Many of the in-class project examples (ad-placement & attention, FinTech adoption, music bias) are **applied** — they target a specific real-world question — while something like "can color palette affect emotional valence" leans more toward **basic** research into a general psychological mechanism. Note: basic research often ends up being applied years later (the textbook's examples: X-rays, vaccines, computer chips all emerged from basic research with no planned application) — so the basic/applied line isn't a judgment of importance.

## 3. Experiment Design as a Cycle (BRSM vs BRED)

Two overlapping cycles were shown:

**BRSM (outer loop):** research question → **report** → **evaluation** → data collection & analysis → back to research question.

**BRED (inner loop, shares the "research question" and "data collection & analysis" nodes):** research question → **problem definition** → **experimental design** → data collection & analysis.

Interpretation: research is **iterative**, not linear — you don't just design once and stop; evaluation of results can send you back to refine the question, and problem definition happens between the question and the actual experimental design.

### Finding Data — the basic requirements
Before designing anything, you need to ask the journalistic questions about your topic and your data:
**Who? What? Where? When? Why? How?**

And you must assess **data quality** — where is the data coming from, is it reliable, is it representative, is it recent/relevant?

## 4. Core Logic of Science: Support vs. Proof

This is one of the most testable conceptual points in the slides.

> **"Scientific research does not prove theories true."**

Research only ever **supports or contradicts** a theory — it never proves it, because there is always an alternative explanation. Correct phrasing to use in scientific writing:
- "*All the evidence so far points towards…*"
- "*The evidence is consistent with…*"

### Worked example: The broccoli allergy
A younger brother gets a rash every Monday. He eats broccoli every Sunday (family dinner). You remove broccoli; the rash doesn't return for 4 weeks.

**Question posed: Has this "proved" broccoli was the problem?**
**Answer: No.**
- If the rash disappears → this is **support for, not proof of**, the broccoli-allergy theory. Alternative explanation: it could have been an herb Gran always cooks alongside the broccoli.
- If the rash *remains* → this gives a more definite result that appears to **rule out** the broccoli theory — though even here there's an outside chance he's allergic to broccoli *and* something else served that day.
- **Key method insight:** by removing one item at a time while holding everything else constant, you isolate the cause — this is essentially describing **experimental control** (manipulate one variable, hold others constant).

### Worked example: Ayurveda and COVID-19 (critical evaluation exercise)
A study gave policemen Ayurvedic medicine, then measured COVID-19 incidence over 6 months and compared it to the general population. Infection rate was lower in the policemen group → researchers concluded Ayurveda prevents/reduces COVID-19 risk.

**How to critique this (general framework, applies broadly to any "should I trust this conclusion" question):**
- **No control/comparability:** Policemen ≠ general population on many other variables (age, fitness, occupational exposure patterns, healthcare access, mask/PPE use, testing rates, baseline health) — any of these, not the medicine, could explain the difference.
- **No random assignment / no proper control group** drawn from the same population and treated identically except for the intervention.
- **Confounds are not ruled out** — this is a correlational/quasi observation, not a controlled experiment, so causal claims ("Ayurveda prevents COVID") are not warranted.
- **What would strengthen the claim:** a randomized controlled trial with a comparable control group (e.g., other policemen not given the medicine), blinding, matched demographics, and controlling for exposure/testing differences.
- This exercise is testing the same "support vs. proof" and "confound" concepts as the broccoli example, just in a real-world/quasi-experimental dress.

### Worked example: Does eating only fish increase IQ?
Shown as a template for the full research cycle:
Research question → **H1** (alternative hypothesis) and **H0** (null hypothesis) → **Experiment** → Result: "no significant difference between groups" → loop back to H1/H0 → **Conclusion: only eating fish does not increase IQ.**
This illustrates the standard **hypothesis-testing pipeline**: pose question → derive testable hypotheses → run experiment → statistical result → conclusion (which itself is only ever "supported" or "not supported," never "proven").

### Worked example: "Heat makes people aggressive" — operationalizing one theory many ways
The class was shown ~6 *different* possible experimental designs to test the *same* theory, illustrating that one theory can be operationalized many ways, each with different trade-offs (control vs realism, causal power vs ethics/feasibility):
1. Have people solve problems in a hot room, then a cold room; measure blood pressure. *(within-subjects, physiological measure)*
2. One group solves problems in a hot room, another in a cool room; have them tear cardboard afterward and observationally rate aggression. *(between-subjects, behavioral measure)*
3. Observe horn-honking by drivers on hot vs. cold days. *(naturalistic observation, field, no manipulation)*
4. Put people in a hot or cold room, then interview using an aggression scale. *(self-report)*
5. Approach people on hot/cold days and administer an aggression scale (if they agree). *(field survey)*
6. Check public crime records for aggression-related crime in hot vs. cold seasons, same city. *(archival research)*

**Takeaway:** these span the spectrum from tightly **controlled lab experiments** (1, 2) to **quasi/field/naturalistic** methods (3, 5) to **archival research** (6) — same research question, very different designs, each with different strengths for internal validity (causal certainty) vs. external validity (real-world generalizability).

### Why science can only ever "support," never "prove" — the deeper reason (confirmation bias & falsifiability)
This grounds the slide's "scientific research does not prove theories true" line in the actual textbook mechanism, and is a natural follow-up exam angle.

- **Confirmation bias:** the tendency to keep testing/collecting evidence that *could* confirm your hypothesis, rather than actively trying to rule it out. Classic illustration: given the sequence "2, 4, 6" and told it fits a hidden rule, most people guess "increasing by 2" and only ever test *other* increasing-by-2 sequences (which keep getting confirmed) — never realizing the actual rule was simply "any three ascending numbers." You can generate confirming evidence for a wrong theory *forever*.
- **The fix — seek disconfirmation (falsification):** the fastest way to test a hypothesis is to try to *break* it, not just support it. This is why a good study design tries to rule out alternative explanations (i.e., it's the same logic as isolating the broccoli/herb confound, or asking what else besides Ayurveda could explain the policemen's lower infection rate).
- **Consequence:** because you can never test *every* possible alternative explanation, and because confirming instances never logically rule out all other theories, a theory is only ever **"supported/confirmed"** or **"unsupported/disconfirmed" — never proven true or false with certainty.** This is exactly the "support vs. proof" language from the slides, just with the underlying reason spelled out.

### Four ways of knowing (where "scientific" evidence sits relative to other sources)
Useful for critically evaluating any claim (e.g., the Ayurveda study, or an ad claiming "scientifically proven"):
1. **Authority** — believing something because an expert/text/institution said so (risk: even experts/institutions can be wrong or biased, e.g., politically-motivated pseudoscience).
2. **Personal experience** — belief from your own observations (risk: overgeneralizing from a small, unrepresentative sample of your own life).
3. **Rationalism** — belief from logic/reasoning alone, without testing it empirically (risk: a logically valid conclusion can still be false if its starting premise was never empirically checked).
4. **Empiricism** — belief grounded in systematic, repeatable, testable observation. This is the backbone of the scientific method and what distinguishes science from non-science.
- **Practical test for "is this scientific?":** does the claim have an **empirical referent** (something observable/measurable, directly or indirectly)? Is it **repeatable** (can another researcher verify it elsewhere)? Is the field **self-correcting** (does it revise theories when new evidence contradicts them)? Claims that fail these (e.g., unfalsifiable claims) aren't scientific, regardless of how "sciency" they sound.
- Note: 
	- **Validity** is about structure. If the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false.
	- **Truth** is about reality. It checks if the premises match the real world.
	- **Soundness** means the argument is valid _and_ the premises are true in fact.

### Observation vs. Inference (Ch.3 — directly underlies the Ayurveda/broccoli-style critique questions)
Two distinct activities happen in any study, and mixing them up is the root of most bad conclusions:
- **Observation:** the raw, accurate recording of what happened (e.g., "policemen who took Ayurvedic medicine had a lower COVID infection rate over 6 months").
- **Inference:** the *interpretation* built on top of the observation (e.g., "therefore Ayurveda prevents COVID"). Inference includes explaining the data, guessing at underlying causes, and building new concepts from it.
- **Key point: observations can be perfectly accurate and repeatable while the inference drawn from them is still wrong**, because the same observation can have multiple possible explanations. Textbook examples:
  - A cockroach that stops responding to "Jump!" after having all its legs removed is observed accurately — but the inference "losing all legs makes a cockroach deaf" is absurd (it can no longer *feel* the vibration cue, it isn't "deaf").
  - A traveler accurately observes that older people in an ethnic neighborhood tend to have stronger accents, but wrongly infers "people develop accents as they age" (rather than: older residents simply immigrated earlier, before English became as dominant).
- **Direct application:** this is exactly the flaw in the Ayurveda/COVID example — the *observation* (lower infection rate in the treated group) may be perfectly real, but the *inference* (Ayurveda caused it) ignores other explanations (occupational differences, demographics, exposure), i.e., confounds. Any time a slide/question asks you to "critique a conclusion," check whether the inference is the *only* explanation consistent with the observation, or just *one* of several.

### Molecular-to-Molar Continuum (Ch.3 — grounds "interdisciplinary relevance" from §1)
Different fields study behavior at different "grain sizes," and none is more "correct" than another — they're complementary:
**Physics (atoms/subatomic) → Chemistry (molecules) → Physiology (body systems) → Psychology (the individual organism's behavior) → Sociology (group behavior) → Ethnology (culture).**
- "Molecular" = smaller/more reductionist unit of analysis; "molar" = larger/more holistic unit. These are *relative* terms — psychology is molecular compared to sociology, but molar compared to chemistry.
- This directly explains why behavioral research slides describe the field as **interdisciplinary** (Psychology, Education, Marketing, Public Health, Neuroscience, HCI, etc.) — each discipline sits at a different point on this continuum, but they all inform each other when studying human behavior.

### Formulating hypotheses & variables (grounds the "operationalize" idea, CO-4)
- A **hypothesis** is an educated, logically-derived prediction (not just "what I think should happen") — good hypotheses have (a) a clear rationale/reasoning for the prediction, (b) an **if–then** structure, (c) a clear statement of the relationship between the variables involved. This is the more detailed version of the H1/H0 boxes in the fish/IQ example.
- **Independent variable (IV):** the condition manipulated/controlled by the researcher (the presumed "cause"). Can be **qualitative** (differs in *kind*, e.g., type of psychotherapy) or **quantitative** (differs in *amount*, e.g., dosage level).
- **Dependent variable (DV):** the behavior/outcome measured, whose changes presumably *depend on* the IV (the presumed "effect"). If a systematic IV→DV relationship is found, that's an empirical/causal (or "functional") relationship.
- This is the concrete mechanism behind "operationalizing" a research question (CO-4): you turn a vague idea ("does heat make people aggressive?") into a specific IV (room temperature: hot vs. cold) and a specific, measurable DV (cardboard-tearing behavior, self-reported aggression score, horn-honking frequency, crime records) — exactly what the six heat-aggression designs in §4 each did differently.

## 6. Research Philosophy (top layer of the Onion)

Four stances on **what counts as reality and how we can know it**, each with characteristic methods:

### Positivism
- Emphasizes **objective, observable facts**: *"I don't want opinions, give me the facts!"*
- Relies heavily on **quantitative methods**.
- Believes in a **single, measurable reality**, studied via scientific observation and experimentation.
- Common in natural sciences, adapted for social science.
- Aligned with the **hypothetico-deductive model**: verify *a priori* hypotheses via experimentation, operationalizing variables/measures; results inform/advance science.
- Focus: identifying **explanatory associations or causal relationships** via quantitative approaches.

### Interpretivism
- Emphasizes the **subjective nature of reality**; understanding social phenomena through participants' own perspectives.
- Associated with **qualitative methods**; prevalent in social sciences.
- Reality is **socially constructed**; understood through in-depth exploration of human experience and meaning.
- Methods: interviews, focus groups, participant observation — rich qualitative data.
- The researcher **co-constructs knowledge** and emphasizes context.

### Pragmatism
- Focuses on **practical outcomes and problem-solving**; "**Whatever works**."
- Concerned with finding **workable solutions** to research questions; useful for applied research with real-world implications.
- Often employs **mixed methods** (quant + qual together).
- Allows drawing from multiple philosophies/methods flexibly, choosing whatever best answers the question at hand.

### Realism
- Seeks to **bridge positivism and interpretivism**.
- Acknowledges an **objective reality** exists, while recognizing our *understanding* of it is shaped by social/historical context.
- Believes in a **stratified reality**: the **empirical** (what we can observe), the **actual** (events that occur, whether observed or not), and the **real** (underlying causal mechanisms)
- Uses a mix of quantitative and qualitative methods to explore both observable phenomena and the deeper structures behind them.
- Useful for understanding complex social systems; informs both theory and practical intervention.

### Worked example — Generative AI & academic assignments (recurring example across all 4 philosophies)
| Topic | Positivist framing (quant, "what/is there an effect") | Interpretivist framing (qual, "how do people experience it") |
|---|---|---|
| Learning outcomes | Does GenAI use improve assignment grades among undergrads? | How do undergrads perceive GenAI's influence on their learning while completing assignments? |
| Critical thinking | Is AI-use frequency associated with critical thinking skills? | How do students describe AI's role in shaping their thinking/problem-solving? |
| Time management | Does AI use reduce time required to complete assignments? | How do students experience the ways AI changes their workflow/time management? |

- **Positivist question:** "Does the frequency of generative AI use improve students' assignment grades?" → measure AI usage frequency + assignment grades → statistical analysis.
- **Interpretivist question:** "How do students experience the use of generative AI while completing academic assignments?" → interviews, reflective journals → thematic analysis.
- **Pragmatist question:** "How does the use of generative AI influence students' learning, assignment quality, and perceptions of learning, and how can universities design effective policies for its use?" — combines *what is happening* (quant: survey 500 students, measure AI usage, compare grades), *why is it happening* (qual: interview 25 students, explore how they actually use AI, identify challenges/benefits), and *what should be done* (practical application/policy).

### Worked example — music streaming research questions, mapped to philosophy
- *"What is the correlation between listening frequency and user demographics?"* → **Positivism** (quantitative, correlational).
- *"How do users construct identity through their playlists?"* → **Interpretivism** (meaning-making, qualitative).
- *"How can listening data and interviews improve recommendation algorithms?"* → **Pragmatism** (practical/applied, mixed methods).
- *"What mechanisms influence music consumption patterns on streaming platforms?"* → **Realism** (underlying causal mechanisms, stratified reality).

**How to recognize philosophy from a question (exam-style skill):** look at the verb/goal.
- "Does X affect/predict/improve Y?" / correlation, comparison of means → **Positivism**.
- "How do people experience/perceive/construct meaning around X?" → **Interpretivism**.
- "How can we use this to solve/design/improve a real system?" → **Pragmatism**.
- "What underlying mechanism explains/produces X?" (bridging observable + deeper structure) → **Realism**.

## 7. Research Approach (second layer of the Onion): Induction, Deduction, Abduction

- **Induction:** Research **starts with observation**, moves to description/analysis of data, in order to **form a theory**. Used when developing a new theory, or in fields with less prior research.
  - *Example:* "What behavioral characteristics define human interaction in cooperative, partially observable communication settings? Can stable, role-specific playing styles be identified from player interactions?"
  - *Example:* "What different styles of binge-watching can be identified from viewing histories?"
- **Deduction:** Research **starts with an existing theory**, then raises a question/hypothesis, collects data to **confirm or reject the hypothesis**. Applied for **testing existing theory**.
  - *Example:* "Does loneliness predict binge-watching frequency?"
  - *Example:* "Does perceived recommendation quality increase binge-watching?"
- **Abduction:** Starts from **observation of an empirical/surprising phenomenon**, then research seeks the **best-guess/most likely explanation** based on available evidence. Moves *between* induction and deduction.
  - *Example:* "Why do Netflix users frequently ignore recommendations that they consider highly accurate?" (a surprising fact in need of the best explanation)

**Quick way to tell them apart:** Deduction = *theory → hypothesis → test*. Induction = *data/observation → theory*. Abduction = *surprising observation → best available explanation* (neither strictly proving nor purely theory-building).

## 8. Research Strategies & Choices (third/fourth layers of the Onion)

### Strategies
- **Survey:** Collects data from many participants using questionnaires or structured interviews.
- **Case Study:** In-depth investigation of a single case, organization, or event (classic historical example referenced: Phineas Gage, the man with the tamping-iron brain injury).
- **Archival Research:** Analyzes existing records, documents, or historical data to answer research questions.
- **Ethnography:** Studies people in their natural environment to understand their culture and behaviors (example image: a large religious gathering — studying group behavior in situ).
- **Grounded Theory:** Generation of hypotheses and theories **from** data (bottom-up). Cyclical/iterative process: **Collect data → Analyze data (code data, write memos, create concept maps) → Theoretical Sampling → Theory**, looping back to collect more data as needed.
  - *Example study:* Weatherall (2000), *"A grounded theory analysis of older adults and information technology,"* Educational Gerontology, 26(4), 371–386. Six older-adult participants interviewed about their experiences/opinions on computer technology; **no hypothesis was tested up front** — themes emerged from the analysis. Findings: participants recognized IT's importance in modern life (motivating exploration); positive attitudes developed/reinforced through direct experience and personal ownership of technology.
- **Experiment:** Tests cause-and-effect relationships by **manipulating variables** under **controlled conditions**.
- **Action Research:** A cyclical, practitioner-driven process (example: an art teacher). Cycle: **Recognize/wonder about a problem in the classroom → Think about possible reasons → Come up with solutions to try → Collect and analyze data → Analyze the solution** → (loop back).

### Research Choices — Mono/Multi/Mixed method design
- **Mono-method:** Uses a **single method** of data collection or analysis to study a phenomenon.
- **Multi-method:** Uses **multiple methods** to study a phenomenon **from different perspectives** — but all methods are from the *same* paradigm (i.e., QUAL + QUAL, or QUANT + QUANT).
- **Mixed-method:** **Combines quantitative and qualitative** methods (QUANT + QUAL) to gain both numerical and in-depth understanding of behavior.

**Distinction to remember:** Multimethod = same type combined (Qual+Qual or Quant+Quant). Mixed methods = different types combined (Quant+Qual).

## 9. Qualitative vs. Quantitative Research

### Qualitative
- Concerned with the **subjective assessment** of qualitative phenomena: attitudes, opinions, behavior.
- Can be a function of the **researcher's insights and impressions**.
- Data is **words, ideas, interviews** — subjective, observes behavior; "experience supports outcome."
- Cannot always be reduced to numbers, though it *can sometimes* be analyzed statistically.
	- Purpose: answer **"Why?"** questions. Data type: observation/symbol/word. Approach: observe and interpret. Analysis: grouping of common data / non-statistical.

### Quantitative
- Applicable to phenomena expressible **in terms of quantity**.
- Requires an **operational definition**: research question → specific definition of the measured variables → generate data → analyze formally/rigorously.
- Data is **numbers, facts** — objective; "numbers support outcome."
- Purpose: answer **"How many/how much?"** Data type: number/statistical result. Approach: measure and test. Analysis: statistical analysis.
- Three quantitative sub-approaches:
  - **Inferential (≈ induction):** form a database from which to infer characteristics or relationships of a population.
  - **Experimental (≈ deduction):** variables are manipulated to observe their effect on other variables.
  - **Simulation:** build mathematical or computational models to study systems.

### Choosing qualitative vs. quantitative (or both)
Framed as an example question: *"Should I select a qualitative or quantitative approach, or both?"* — e.g., for understanding **why users interact with an interface in certain ways** (this "why" framing signals qualitative), versus reading raw sensor/instrument values like an air-quality monitor's PM2.5/AQI/HCHO/TVOC readings (this framing signals quantitative).

**Different approaches complement each other** — three possible orderings shown:
1. Qualitative → Quantitative → Results (explore first, then measure/confirm)
2. Quantitative → Qualitative → Results (measure first, then explain the "why" behind the numbers)
3. Qualitative + Quantitative → Results (in parallel/combined)

**Mixed Methods = Qualitative Research + Quantitative Research.**

The three-camp cartoon (useful mnemonic): 
- "**Qualitative methods are best**" camp → associated with **constructivism, inductive logic, exploratory research**.
- "**The research question drives everything**" (middle, mixed-methods) camp → associated with **pragmatism, abductive logic, QUAL and QUANT analysis**.
- "**Quantitative methods are best**" camp → associated with **postpositivism, deductive logic, confirmatory research**.

This maps back cleanly onto §6–7: Qualitative↔Interpretivism↔Induction; Quantitative↔Positivism↔Deduction; Mixed↔Pragmatism↔Abduction.

## 10. How the Layers Connect (synthesis / likely exam framing)

A single research question can be approached at every level of the onion simultaneously — you should be able to state, for any given research question:
1. **Philosophy** it reflects (positivist / interpretivist / pragmatist / realist)
2. **Approach** it reflects (inductive / deductive / abductive)
3. **Strategy** best suited to it (survey / experiment / case study / ethnography / archival / grounded theory / action research)
4. **Choice** of method (mono / multi / mixed)
5. **Data type** implied (qualitative / quantitative / both) and **why**

**Worked template (from the AI-in-education example):**
- "Does GenAI use improve grades?" → Positivism → Deduction (testing a specific effect) → Survey/quasi-experiment → Mono-method (quant) → Quantitative (grades, usage frequency; statistical analysis).
- "How do students experience GenAI while completing assignments?" → Interpretivism → Induction (theory-building from lived experience) → Interviews/ethnographic → Mono-method (qual) → Qualitative (interviews, reflective journals; thematic analysis).
- "How does GenAI influence learning/quality/perception, and how should universities design policy?" → Pragmatism → Abduction (moving between the two to find the most useful/complete answer) → Mixed strategy (survey + interviews) → **Mixed-method** → Both quantitative (survey of 500, usage vs. grades) and qualitative (interview 25 students on challenges/benefits) feeding into a practical/policy recommendation.

## 11. Quick-Reference Glossary

- **Behavioral research:** interdisciplinary study of observable behavior and inferred mental processes, using quantitative and/or qualitative methods.
- **Operationalization:** defining how to measure a phenomenon that isn't directly measurable.
- **H1 / H0:** alternative / null hypothesis.
- **Research Onion:** layered model — Philosophy → Approach → Strategy → Choices → Time Horizon → Techniques & Procedures.
- **Positivism:** objective single reality, quantitative, hypothesis-testing.
- **Interpretivism:** subjective/socially-constructed reality, qualitative, meaning-focused.
- **Pragmatism:** practical problem-solving, mixed methods, "whatever works."
- **Realism:** objective reality but socially-shaped understanding; stratified (empirical/actual/real); mixed methods.
- **Induction:** observation → theory.
- **Deduction:** theory → hypothesis → test.
- **Abduction:** surprising observation → best available explanation.
- **Mono-method:** one method. **Multi-method:** multiple methods, same paradigm (all qual or all quant). **Mixed-method:** quant + qual combined.
- **Grounded theory:** theory emerges from iterative coding of data (no prior hypothesis).
- **Action research:** cyclical practitioner-led problem-solving (recognize → think → solve → collect/analyze → repeat).
- **Ethnography:** studying people in their natural setting.
- **Archival research:** using existing records/historical data.
- **Cross-sectional vs. longitudinal:** one-time snapshot vs. data over time.
- **Internal validity:** confidence that the manipulated variable, not a confound, caused the effect (highest in controlled experiments).
- **External validity / generalizability:** how well findings extend beyond the specific study sample/setting.
- **Confound / extraneous variable:** an uncontrolled variable that offers an alternative explanation for results (e.g., the herbs in the broccoli example; demographic/exposure differences in the Ayurveda example).
- **Science does not prove theories true** — it only provides evidence that supports or contradicts them.
- **Confirmation bias:** favoring evidence that confirms a hypothesis over actively seeking evidence that could disconfirm it.
- **Falsifiability:** a theory must, in principle, be testable/disprovable by evidence to count as scientific; the fastest way to test a hypothesis is to try to break it, not just support it.
- **Empirical referent:** something observable/measurable (directly or indirectly) — a requirement for a question to be studied scientifically.
- **Four ways of knowing:** authority, personal experience, rationalism, empiricism — science relies mainly on the last.
- **Independent variable (IV):** the condition manipulated by the researcher (presumed cause); qualitative (differs in kind) or quantitative (differs in amount).
- **Dependent variable (DV):** the measured behavior/outcome, presumed to depend on the IV (presumed effect).
- **Hypothesis:** a specific, logically-derived, testable prediction, ideally stated in if–then form with a clear rationale.
- **Goals of science:** understanding (description → explanation), prediction, control (control only possible when the phenomenon can be manipulated).
- **Determinism:** the assumption that behavior is lawful/orderly, i.e., a function of prior events, not random.
- **Basic vs. applied research:** basic = understanding for its own sake, no immediate practical goal; applied = aimed at a specific real-world problem. Basic research often becomes applied later.
- **Observation vs. inference:** observation = accurate raw recording of what happened; inference = the interpretation/explanation built on top. Observations can be accurate while the inference drawn from them is still wrong (confounds/alternative explanations).
- **Molecular-to-molar continuum:** different disciplines (physics → chemistry → physiology → psychology → sociology → ethnology) study behavior at different, complementary grain sizes.

## 12. Research Types: How a Study Can Be Classified

Three independent dichotomies used to classify any research question. A single study can be described on all three axes at once — they are separate spectrums, not alternatives to the Research Onion of §6–9.

### Descriptive vs. Analytical

- **Descriptive:** describes the state of affairs as it currently exists — fact-finding. Helps identify patterns and generate hypotheses for later study. The researcher has **no control over variables**; can only report what is/was happening. **Correlational, not causal** — doesn't establish cause–effect, so follow-up experimental/quasi-experimental work may be needed. Common in social sciences and business studies. _Example: tracking Xiaomi's rising market share vs. Samsung in India over time — a chart of "what happened."_
- **Analytical:** the researcher critically evaluates already-available material/data by analyzing facts and information. Focused on explaining _why_, not just reporting _what_.
- **Memorable pairing (epidemiology):** Descriptive = **"What"** (who/when/where does something occur; generates hypotheses; smaller/less complex; uses individuals or a group; case reports, case series, incidence counts). Analytical = **"Why"** (measures the association between an exposure and an outcome using individual-level data; tests hypotheses about determinants/risk factors; larger/more complex; uses comparison groups; observational + experimental studies).

### Applied vs. Fundamental

- **Applied:** aimed at finding a solution to an **immediate problem** facing a society, industry, business, or medical community. _Example: an AI-for-social-good research center pairing AI methods with a concrete social problem; a project using social-network analysis to identify influential homeless youth so HIV-prevention information spreads through their peer network._
- **Fundamental** (a.k.a. **"Pure"/"Basic" research**, same idea as §1's Basic vs. Applied Research): _"gathering knowledge for the sake of knowledge."_ Concerned with generalizations and formulating theory with a broad base of potential application — now or later — adding to the existing organized body of scientific knowledge. _Example: pure mathematics._
- **Mnemonic pairing shown in class:** music neuroscience (how the brain processes music) = fundamental; music therapy (using music to treat patients) = applied.

### Conceptual vs. Empirical

- **Conceptual:** related to abstract idea(s) or theory. Systematically developing new ideas, analyzing abstract theories, or reinterpreting existing principles through structured scholarly thinking. Associated with philosophers. _Example figure used in class: Aristotle, "developing new concepts or theories."_
- **Empirical:** relies on **experimentation**; data-based research; can reach conclusions without a specific need for pre-existing theory. Associated with experimenters/inventors ("Edison," testing to form new theories or confirm old ones). _Example figure: profiling/benchmarking C++ code — you run it and observe what actually happens, rather than reasoning about it abstractly._
- **Class discussion prompt:** Newton sitting under the apple tree — is discovering gravity from watching an apple fall conceptual or empirical? The intended takeaway is that it's **both**: an empirical observation (the apple falling) triggers conceptual theorizing (universal gravitation). Real research usually blends both ends of the axis rather than sitting purely at one pole.

### Why these classifications matter for research design

|Classification|Main question|Consequence for design|
|---|---|---|
|Descriptive|What is happening?|Describe patterns|
|Analytical|What explains/relates to what?|Test relationships/mechanisms|
|Fundamental|Why does this matter theoretically?|Build knowledge/theory|
|Applied|What problem can this address?|Develop solutions/interventions|
|Conceptual|What can we reason about theoretically?|Develop models/frameworks|
|Empirical|What does evidence show?|Collect/analyze observations|

These three axes cut across the Research Onion of §6–9: e.g. Positivism tends to pair with Analytical + Empirical + Deductive work; Interpretivism tends to pair with Descriptive-of-meaning + Conceptual-leaning + Inductive work; Applied/Fundamental cuts across both philosophies (you can do applied-positivist or applied-interpretivist work equally).

### The Wheel of Research

A circular (cyclical, not linear) model of how a full research project moves through stages, built up piece-by-piece across several slides.

**Top half (clockwise from top): theory, ideas → models → concepts → hypotheses → operationalization → data collection.** **Bottom half (continuing clockwise): data collection → descriptions, typologies → data analysis → explanation → generalization → assessing reliability → practical implications** → back up to theory, ideas.

Two labelled entry points onto the same circle:

- **Typical starting point for quantitative research** = enters at the **top** ("theory") — you start from an existing theory, derive hypotheses, then collect data to test them. This is the **deductive** route (theory → data), matching §7.
- **Typical starting point for qualitative research** = enters at the **bottom** ("empirical data") — you start by collecting/observing data and build toward theory. This is the **inductive** route (data → theory), matching §7.

A more detailed version of the same wheel is just this circle re-drawn as an explicit loop of concrete steps: **research question → problem definition → experimental design → data collection and analysis → evaluation → report** → back to research question — the same BRSM/BRED cycle already introduced in §3.

The wheel is also labelled with the three dichotomies above sitting around its outside: **Applied / Fundamental** and **Conceptual / Empirical** label the top half (the theory-facing side); **Analytical / Descriptive** label the bottom half (the data-facing side) — visually reinforcing that fundamental/conceptual work lives closer to "theory, ideas," while descriptive/analytical work lives closer to "data analysis, descriptions."

**Key takeaway:** research is a _cycle_, not a one-way street — practical implications and reliability-assessment at the bottom feed back up into refining theory and ideas at the top, matching the "research is iterative" point already made about BRSM/BRED in §3.

## 13. Beginning of Research, Part II: Active Reading and the Literature Review

(Extends §2's "Research Onion — where do you start?" with the concrete first-weeks workflow, from Lecture 4.)

### Becoming an active (critical) reader/listener

|Non-critical reading|Critical reading|
|---|---|
|passive|active|
|just the facts|asks _what, how, why_|
|gullible|skeptical|
|reactive|purposeful|

When reading or listening to any piece of technical work, ask:

- **Where** did the author seem to draw the ideas from?
- **What** exactly was accomplished by this piece of work?
- **How** does it relate to other work in the field?
- **What** would be a reasonable next step to build on this work?
- **What** ideas from related fields might be brought to bear on this subject?

### Turning reading into a literature review (the practical habit)

- **Log** your technical reading/listening as you go — notes on what you read, when, and answers to the questions above.
- This log **ends up being your literature review** for your study/project/thesis — far easier to write from an ongoing log than to reconstruct from memory later.
- Logging also makes **citing easier**, and a **periodic review** of the log helps ideas "begin to fit together" as patterns across papers become visible.
- **Repeat this after a few months** — re-reading your own older notes with fresh eyes often surfaces connections you missed the first time.
- **Myth to avoid:** _"Finish literature review and then start [research]."_ In practice, literature review and research design/execution run in parallel and feed each other — reading doesn't stop once you start experimenting.
- Staying an "active" reader/listener throughout the project also makes it easier to generate **alternative topics/directions** if the original plan needs to change.

### How do you find relevant literature?

- Keyword searches — start broad, narrow with additional/combined keywords. _(Textbook illustration, Ch.4: searching PsycINFO for "television" → 6,671 hits; "television violence" → 156; "television children" → 17 — narrowing the keyword set narrows the hit count to something skimmable.)_
- Tutorials (learning the tools/field basics).
- **Citation chaining** — follow references backward from a paper you like, and forward via "cited by" to see who later built on it.
- Systematic reviews / meta-analyses — a single paper that has already aggregated a field for you.
- Discussing with peers.
- Discussions on online forums and social media — **use with caution**, quality/credibility varies.
- Research groups — your own lab, or others working in the area.
- _(Textbook additionally lists: professors — including outside your own university; textbooks, checking the subject index for your topic; and library databases such as PsycLit, PsycINFO, ProQuest, JSTOR Scholarly Journals, MedLine, and Sociological Abstracts.)_

### How do you read a research paper?

A paper has a standard anatomy — knowing it lets you read strategically instead of start-to-finish: **Title → Authors → Author affiliations → Abstract → Introduction → Materials and methods → Author summary → Results (with figures) → Discussion → Conclusion → Acknowledgements → Author contributions → References.** A common efficient reading order: Title → Abstract → Figures/Results → Discussion/Conclusion → (only then, if still relevant) Introduction and Methods in full.

### What is a research gap?

A research gap is **the missing piece in existing knowledge that your study aims to fill** — visualized in class as the one missing jigsaw piece surrounded by pieces representing "existing studies" and "known findings," with "limitations" pointing at the gap.

**Types of research gap** (two overlapping lists were shown in class; combined here as one reference set):

1. **Knowledge gap** — a broad lack of understanding/information on a topic, often because it's new, overlooked, or under-studied.
2. **Theoretical gap** — existing theories fail to adequately explain a phenomenon, or no suitable conceptual framework exists.
3. **Methodological gap** — existing studies relied on outdated, inappropriate, or narrow methods, suggesting a need for more robust or alternative approaches.
4. **Evidence gap** — a lack of strong, credible, high-quality findings to support claims, often from poor study design or insufficient data quality.
5. **Empirical gap** — theory exists, but little or no real-world observational/experimental data validates it.
6. **Population gap** — specific groups/demographics/communities are underrepresented in existing studies, leading to incomplete or biased findings.
7. **Geographical gap** — research is concentrated in certain regions/environments/contexts, leaving others unstudied.
8. **Practical gap** — research findings aren't applied in real-world practice — a disconnect between academic insight and implementation.
9. **Time gap** — documenting troubleshooting steps, solutions, and new knowledge as it's gained (a gap in what's actually been recorded over time).
10. **Evidence–practice gap** — strong evidence exists but isn't adopted in practice, due to barriers in knowledge transfer, policy, or industry adoption.

### Formulating the research problem

- Initially, the problem is stated in a broad, general way — you have to **"formulate a question(s)."**
- **Understand the problem thoroughly.**
- **Narrow it down** and phrase it in **operational terms** — turn the vague idea into something you can actually measure (the same "operationalization" step from the Wheel of Research above, and from the IV/DV discussion in §4).
- Once identified:
    - Be aware of the literature in the area.
    - Identify what is **different** between your work and others' — effectively, "find your gap" (see above).
    - Assess **feasibility**: feasibility = _f_(time, resources).
- _(Textbook, Ch.4, adds the reasons a research problem gets selected in the first place: to evaluate or advance a theory; to compare two or more competing theories; to address a real applied/practical problem; to resolve inconsistent or contradictory findings across existing studies — e.g. some studies find sleep-after-learning helps memory while others find it hurts, so new research compares the studies' procedures to find the source of the disagreement; or to extend/replicate prior findings — same effect in a new population or condition, or the same method repeated to check a surprising result holds up.)_

### After formulating the problem — write a brief summary that:

- States it **clearly and concisely**.
- Establishes **significance** — not trivial, not a repeat of previous work; if it _is_ a repeat, **justify why** (e.g. replication to check an implausible finding, or extension to a new population/condition — see textbook reasons above).
- **Delineates** (limits the scope) so it's practically investigable.
- Confirms it's **possible to obtain** the information/tools needed to explore the problem.
- Confirms it's **possible to draw conclusions** related to the problem — the point of research is to find some answers, i.e. you should be able to state potential hypotheses.
- Throughout, **stay an "active" reader/listener** — it makes it easier to generate alternative topics if the original plan doesn't pan out.

## 14. Exploratory vs. Hypothesis-Based Research

|                     | **Hypothesis-based (confirmatory)**                                            | **Exploratory**                                                                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Basis               | Based on scientific theory; finds out if the theory is supported by the facts  | Backed by no, or little-known, theory                                                                  |
| Motivating question | Can I reject H₀?                                                               | How can I optimally explain/describe variation in my data set?                                         |
| What it does        | Makes specific statements about causal relationships among a few key phenomena | Driven by hope and the chance of discovery                                                             |
| Method flavor       | —                                                                              | **Data mining**: post-hoc analyses, explanations, and hypotheses generated _after_ looking at the data |
| Reception           | Liked by funding/publishing agencies — clean, testable claims                  | Risk: a finding may not be a genuine new discovery, or may be a false positive found by chance         |

- The two are **not fully separate** — they form a cycle: **exploratory research** (pictured in class as Sherlock-Holmes-style clue-following) generates a **potential cause–effect** idea → this becomes a hypothesis fed into **confirmatory/hypothesis-based research** → whose result generates **new knowledge to explore**, looping back into further exploratory work. Exploratory findings often _become_ tomorrow's hypotheses, and confirmatory results often open up _new_ exploratory questions.
- **Can exploratory studies have a hypothesis?** The slides pose this as an open discussion question. The consistent answer, given the Grounded Theory approach already covered in §8 ("no hypothesis was tested up front — themes emerged from the analysis"), is that exploratory work does **not require** a hypothesis going in, though a loose research **problem** (§13) still frames what you're looking for.
- Related quote used in class (Einstein, paraphrased): work that already knows exactly what it's doing in advance would not really be called "research."

## 15. Hypotheses in Depth

### What is a hypothesis? (two textbook-style definitions shown in class)

1. **A proposition made as a basis for reasoning, without any assumption of its truth** — a "reasonable guess."
2. **A supposition or proposed explanation made on the basis of limited evidence, as a starting point for further investigation.**

_(Textbook, Ch.4, gives the practical version: a hypothesis is your educated guess about what should happen in a given situation under certain conditions. It should derive logically from prior findings or a theory — never just from what you personally think should happen. Good hypotheses have a clear rationale, an if–then structure, and a clear statement of the relationship between the variables involved, per §4's IV/DV material. Not every study needs a hypothesis — purely exploratory/descriptive work may not have one going in, §14.)_

A research problem funnels down into a hypothesis, which is typically split into two complementary statements:

- **H₀ (null hypothesis):** the hypothesis "deemed true until proven wrong" by the experimental data — the _no-effect / no-difference / no-relationship_ statement.
- **H₁ or Hₐ (alternative hypothesis):** the statement that there _is_ an effect/difference/relationship.

**Worked example (bio-fertilizer):**

- H₀: application of bio-fertilizer 'x' does **not** increase plant growth.
- H₁: application of bio-fertilizer 'x' **does** increase plant growth.

**Practice examples (writing an H₀ from a question):**

| Research question                                                        | Null hypothesis (H₀)                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Are adults doing better at mathematics than teenagers?                   | Mathematical ability does not depend on age                 |
| Does the risk of a heart attack reduce with daily aspirin intake?        | A heart attack is not affected by the daily dose of aspirin |
| Are teenagers using cell phones to access the internet more than elders? | _(left as an in-class exercise)_                            |
| Are cats concerned about their food color?                               | _(left as an in-class exercise)_                            |

### Directional vs. non-directional hypotheses

Once there's reason to expect _some_ effect (i.e. to reject H₀), you can further specify **which direction** you expect:

- **Directional (one-tailed):** predicts the direction of the effect.
- **Non-directional (two-tailed):** predicts only that _some_ effect exists, without predicting its direction.

**Worked examples (same H₀ / directional-Hₐ / non-directional-Hₐ pattern each time):**

|Topic|H₀|Hₐ (directional)|Hₐ (non-directional)|
|---|---|---|---|
|Age & COVID mortality|Mortality rate is not dependent on age|Mortality rate of old people is greater than young people|Mortality rate affects age groups differently|
|Gender & COVID|COVID effects are not dependent on gender|COVID affects women less than men|COVID affects women and men differently|
|IDE & coding efficiency|Use of software X is not related to coding efficiency|Using software X improves coding efficiency|Using software X affects coding efficiency|

**Reading tip:** a directional hypothesis uses a comparative verb ("greater than," "less than," "improves"); a non-directional hypothesis uses a neutral verb ("affects," "differently," "is related to") — this exact wording difference is what a one-tailed vs. two-tailed statistical test later corresponds to.

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- §4's fish/IQ worked example (H1 vs H0 → experiment → "no significant difference between groups" → conclusion "only eating fish does not increase IQ") is a worked instance of _failing to reject_ H₀.
- §6/§9's Positivism, §7's Deduction, and quantitative research all converge on hypothesis-based (confirmatory) work; §8's Grounded Theory and §14's exploratory research converge on the no-hypothesis-up-front, data-first end of the spectrum.

## 16. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 4)

- **Descriptive research:** reports the current state of affairs; fact-finding; correlational, not causal; generates hypotheses.
- **Analytical research:** critically evaluates existing data/facts to explain _why_, not just _what_.
- **Fundamental (basic/pure) research:** knowledge for its own sake; broad generalizations/theory; same idea as "basic research" in §1's glossary entry.
- **Conceptual research:** developing/reinterpreting abstract ideas or theory through scholarly reasoning, without necessarily running an experiment.
- **Empirical research:** data-based, relies on experimentation; can reach conclusions without a pre-existing theory to test.
- **Wheel of Research:** cyclical model — theory/ideas → models → concepts → hypotheses → operationalization → data collection → descriptions/typologies → data analysis → explanation → generalization → assessing reliability → practical implications → back to theory. Quantitative research typically enters at "theory" (deductive); qualitative research typically enters at "empirical data" (inductive).
- **Research gap:** the missing piece in existing knowledge a study aims to fill; types include knowledge, theoretical, methodological, evidence, empirical, population, geographical, practical, time, and evidence–practice gaps.
- **Literature review:** the accumulated log of active/critical reading over time, periodically revisited; not a one-time step done before research starts, but an ongoing parallel activity.
- **Citation chaining:** following a paper's references backward, and its "cited by" list forward, to find related literature.
- **Exploratory research:** data-first, no (or little) prior theory; driven by chance of discovery; findings feed into future hypotheses; risk of false positives/data mining.
- **Hypothesis-based (confirmatory) research:** theory-first; tests whether facts support a specific, pre-stated hypothesis; motivating question is "can I reject H0?"
- **Null hypothesis (H0):** the no-effect/no-difference/no-relationship statement, treated as true until the data justify rejecting it.
- **Alternative hypothesis (H1/Ha):** the statement that an effect/difference/relationship exists; can be directional (predicts the direction, one-tailed) or non-directional (predicts only that an effect exists, two-tailed).
- **Feasibility (of a research problem):** a function of time and available resources — feasibility = f(time, resources).

## 17. Significance Testing & Hypothesis Testing

### The core question

Hypothesis testing exists to answer one practical question: **how sure are you that the results you found in an experiment are actually true**, rather than a fluke of the particular sample you happened to collect?

### What is significance testing?

- **Significance testing is about finding the probability that a result would have occurred by chance if there is, in fact, no effect** — i.e., if the null hypothesis (H₀, §15) is actually true.
- Picture a bell curve of all possible results **under the assumption that H₀ is true**. The peak is the **true value under the null hypothesis and most likely observation**. Results far out in either tail are **very unlikely observations** if H₀ is really true.
- A **95% statistical significance threshold** marks off the outer edge of that curve (conventionally the most common threshold, though not the only one used). If your **observed result** falls beyond that threshold — out in the unlikely tail — the area of the curve beyond it is your **observed p-value (statistical significance)**.
- Intuition: if your result would be very unlikely to occur by chance when there's truly no effect, but you _did_ observe it, that's evidence the "no effect" assumption (H₀) is probably wrong.

### The p-value

- **A p-value is a measure of the probability that an observed difference could have occurred just by random chance, assuming the null hypothesis is true.**
- **The lower the p-value, the greater the statistical significance of the observed difference.**
- **A p-value is a measure of how much evidence you have _against_ H₀** — it is not a measure of how _big_ or how _important_ the effect is, and it is not the probability that H₀ is true.
- **Rough interpretation scale** (shown in class as a tongue-in-cheek chart, useful as a memory aid for how p-values are conventionally read):

|p-value|Conventional interpretation|
|---|---|
|≤ 0.001|Highly significant|
|0.01–0.03|Highly significant|
|0.04–0.049|Significant|
|0.050|"Redo calculations" (right at the boundary)|
|0.051–0.06|"On the edge of significance"|
|0.07–0.09|"Highly suggestive, significant at the p<0.10 level"|
|≥ 0.1|Not significant ("interesting subgroup analysis" — i.e., often a sign of going looking for _some_ significant result after the fact, a data-mining/false-positive risk connecting back to §14's Exploratory Research caution)|

- **Practical exam skill — "reject or not reject H₀?":** worked example: _"There is a relationship between age and job satisfaction."_ Framed as: H₀ = "there is **no** relationship between age and job satisfaction." If your significance test gives you a low enough p-value (below your threshold, e.g., 0.05), the decision is **reject H₀** ("Yes!" — there does appear to be a relationship). If the p-value is above threshold, the decision is **fail to reject H₀** — you don't have enough evidence to say the relationship exists, though this does **not** prove H₀ is true (same "support, not proof" logic as §4).
- **Population vs. sample:** significance testing exists precisely because you almost never have access to the whole **population** — you only have a **sample** drawn from it. The p-value quantifies how confident you can be that a pattern seen in your _sample_ reflects something real in the _population_, rather than being an artifact of which particular people happened to end up in your sample.

### The two ways a decision can go wrong: Type I and Type II errors

Hypothesis testing is framed as a 2×2 decision table crossing **your decision based on the sample** against **the (unknown, in practice) truth about the population**:

|                       | **H₀ true (in reality)**                                                                                                          | **Hₐ true (in reality)**                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reject H₀**         | **Type I error (α)** — "false positive": you conclude there's an effect when there really isn't one                               | **Correct decision — Specificity (1−α)**: you correctly detect a real effect _(note: the slide labels this cell "Specificity," though this cell is more standardly called statistical power/a true positive; see the note below)_ |
| **Fail to reject H₀** | **Correct decision — Sensitivity/Power (1−β)** _(as labelled in the slide; standard terminology would call this a true negative)_ | **Type II error (β)** — "false negative": you fail to detect an effect that's really there                                                                                                                                        |

**A note on the slide's labelling vs. standard statistical terminology:** the class slide's placement of "Specificity" and "Sensitivity/Power" swaps the usual convention (normally Power/Sensitivity = correctly rejecting H₀ when Hₐ is true, and Specificity = correctly failing to reject H₀ when H₀ is true). Learn the table exactly as your instructor presented it for the exam, but be aware that in most textbooks and in the diagnostic-test framing below, **Power (Sensitivity)** refers to correctly detecting a true effect/true positive, and **Specificity** refers to correctly identifying a true negative. If a question asks you to define these terms rather than just fill in the class's table, use the standard definitions:

- **α (alpha):** the Type I error rate — the probability of rejecting H₀ when it's actually true. This is exactly the significance threshold (e.g., 0.05) you set before running the test.
- **β (beta):** the Type II error rate — the probability of failing to reject H₀ when Hₐ is actually true.
- **Power = 1 − β:** the probability of correctly detecting a real effect when one exists.

**Memory aid:** Type I error is like a smoke alarm going off when there's no fire (false alarm); Type II error is like a smoke alarm staying silent while the house is actually burning (missed detection). Which error is "worse" depends on context — e.g., in a medical test, a Type II error (missing a real disease) is often far more costly than a Type I error (a false alarm that gets ruled out on follow-up testing).

### The confusion-matrix version (diagnostic-test framing)

The same 2×2 logic is re-drawn using a medical diagnostic-test analogy — mapping "test result" against "actual disease status":

| |**Disease present**|**Disease absent**|
|---|---|---|
|**Test positive**|**TP** (True Positive)|**FP** (False Positive)|
|**Test negative**|**FN** (False Negative)|**TN** (True Negative)|

This maps directly onto the hypothesis-testing table: "test positive" ≈ "reject H₀," "disease present" ≈ "Hₐ true." So **FP ≈ Type I error**, **FN ≈ Type II error**.

**Derived metrics** (all built from TP/FP/FN/TN) — useful vocabulary for reading papers or being asked to define a term on the exam:

|Metric|Formula|What it captures|
|---|---|---|
|**False Positive Rate (FPR)**|FP / (TN + FP)|Of everyone who's actually H₀-true/disease-free, what fraction got a false alarm|
|**Specificity (TNR)**|1 − FPR|Of everyone who's actually H₀-true/disease-free, what fraction were correctly cleared|
|**Power / Recall / Sensitivity**|TP / (TP + FN) = 1 − Type II error rate|Of everyone who actually has the effect/disease, what fraction did you correctly catch|
|**False Omission Rate (FOR)**|FN / (TN + FN)|Of everyone you _cleared_ (failed to reject H₀ / tested negative), what fraction were actually wrong|
|**False Discovery Rate (FDR)**|FP / (FP + TP)|Of everyone you _flagged_ (rejected H₀ / tested positive), what fraction were actually wrong|
|**Precision**|1 − FDR|Of everyone you flagged, what fraction were correctly flagged|

**Key distinction to keep straight for the exam:** **α/Type I/FPR-side metrics** are about your **false-alarm rate among true negatives**; **β/Type II/Power-side metrics** are about your **miss rate (or catch rate) among true positives**. A significance threshold (e.g., p < 0.05) directly controls α — it does **not** directly control β/power; power depends additionally on sample size and effect size, which is why "the study is well-powered" and "the result is statistically significant" are two different claims.

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- This section is the formal statistical machinery underneath §15's H₀/H₁ material — §15 taught you how to _write_ a null and alternative hypothesis; this section teaches you how a study actually _decides_ whether to reject that H₀ once data comes in.
- The **"support, not proof"** principle from §4 is exactly why failing to reject H₀ is phrased as "fail to reject," never "H₀ is proven true" — a non-significant result is just insufficient evidence against H₀, not positive proof that there's truly no effect (this is precisely a potential **Type II error** situation: maybe there _is_ an effect, but your study didn't have the power to detect it).
- The **p ≥ 0.1 "interesting subgroup analysis"** joke in the interpretation table is a direct callback to the **confirmation bias / data-mining** risk discussed in §4 and §14 — going back into your data to find _some_ subgroup where the result looks significant, after the main test came back non-significant, is a classic way to manufacture false positives.

## 18. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 5)

- **Significance testing:** the process of finding the probability that an observed result would occur by chance if the null hypothesis (H₀) is true.
- **p-value:** the probability that an observed difference could have occurred by random chance, assuming H₀ is true; lower p-values indicate stronger evidence against H₀. Not a measure of effect size or the probability H₀ is true.
- **Significance threshold (α):** the p-value cutoff (conventionally 0.05, i.e., a 95% significance threshold) below which a result is considered statistically significant enough to reject H₀.
- **Type I error (α, false positive):** rejecting H₀ when it is actually true — concluding there's an effect when there isn't one.
- **Type II error (β, false negative):** failing to reject H₀ when Hₐ is actually true — missing a real effect.
- **Power (1 − β):** the probability of correctly detecting a real effect (correctly rejecting H₀ when Hₐ is true); depends on sample size, effect size, and the chosen significance threshold, not on the threshold alone.
- **Specificity (1 − FPR / True Negative Rate):** the proportion of true negatives (H₀-true cases) correctly identified as such.
- **Sensitivity / Recall:** the proportion of true positives (Hₐ-true cases) correctly identified as such; equivalent to power.
- **False Positive Rate (FPR):** proportion of true-negative cases incorrectly flagged as positive.
- **False Discovery Rate (FDR):** proportion of all positive (rejected-H₀) results that are actually false positives; Precision = 1 − FDR.
- **False Omission Rate (FOR):** proportion of all negative (failed-to-reject-H₀) results that are actually false negatives.
- **Confusion matrix (TP/FP/FN/TN):** a 2×2 table cross-tabulating a test's decision against ground truth, from which all the above rates are derived; maps directly onto the H₀/Hₐ hypothesis-testing decision table.

## 19. Designing an Experiment

### Starting point for designing an experiment

Every experiment design starts from the same three moves, sitting on top of a stated **hypothesis** and clear **objectives** (pictured in class as a "puzzle-piece path to objectives"):

1. **Identify Variables** — independent, dependent, controlled (see below).
2. **Identify Conditions** — the specific levels/groups being compared (e.g., three groups being weighed, illustrating that repeated/averaged measurement reduces random error).
3. **Identify Setting** — lab/controlled vs. naturalistic/field (binoculars vs. lab-door icons used in class to represent this choice).

### Independent and dependent variables — recap and drill

This restates and drills §5's IV/DV material with new worked examples:

- **Independent variable (IV):** what you change/manipulate.
- **Dependent variable (DV):** what you observe/measure.
- **Controlled variable:** what you keep the same across conditions.

**Class drill examples (identify IV/DV for each):**

- _"The influence of the color of the cup on consumers' perception of a hot beverage"_ (Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence) → IV = cup color; DV = perceived taste/temperature of the beverage.
- _"Severity of gambling is associated with severity of depressive symptoms in pathological gamblers"_ (Thomsen et al.) → note the _"associated with"_ phrasing — this is a **correlational** study title, not an experimental one, so there's no manipulated IV in the strict sense; gambling severity and depressive symptom severity are both measured variables.
- _Teaching efficacy ↔ students' interest level_ (image: one student asleep, one engaged) → IV = teaching efficacy/method; DV = student interest level; **Extraneous variable?** — things like how much sleep each student got, their existing interest in the subject, or time of day could all masquerade as an effect of teaching efficacy.

### Extraneous variables

**Extraneous variables** are undesirable variables that influence the relationship between the variables an experimenter is examining — they affect the outcome of an experiment even though they aren't the variables actually of interest. Three types:

|Type|Definition|Example|
|---|---|---|
|**Random**|Creates _unpredictable_ error in measurement; unsystematic|A thermostat going on/off unpredictably; experimental apparatus behaving slightly differently trial to trial; a watering can watering a plant unevenly|
|**Constant (systematic)**|A source of constant/systematic error that causes measurements to deviate _consistently_ from their true value|A clinical trial where all participants happen to be tested only during the day, or an AC unit that's only running in one of two buildings being compared (Mon vs. Tue conditions)|
|**Confounding**|An extraneous variable that changes **systematically along with the IV** you're studying — it therefore offers an **alternative explanation** for your results|Campaign ad content (IV) → Vote (DV), confounded by political preference, gender, knowledge of issues, religion, home community, ethnicity, age — any of which could independently predict the vote|

**Why confounds matter — three specific failure modes:**

- You may **assume the IV has an effect when it doesn't** (the confound was actually driving the result).
- You may **assume an effect doesn't occur when it does** (the confound masked a real IV effect).
- You may **wrongly identify the causal component of the IV** (you attribute the effect to the wrong part of what you manipulated).

**How to avoid confounds (the practical checklist):**

- Manipulate **only** the IV; control all other variables, so that any change in the DV must be due to the IV.
- Provide an **identical setting/treatment** for all subjects: standardized instructions and procedures, same environment, etc.
- **Don't give hints** about the "right" way of responding (this connects to Demand Characteristics, below).

_(Textbook, Ch.1, gives a canonical illustration of exactly this failure mode: two day-care centers are compared, one shown a violent TV program and one a nonviolent program, with aggressive behavior afterward as the DV. Even if the two centers differ significantly on aggression, you cannot conclude the TV content caused it — the centers likely differ in staff quality, activities, typical programming, and parents' income/education, any of which is a confound offering an alternative explanation. This is the same logic as the campaign-ad and Ayurveda/COVID examples in §4.)_

### Comparison studies

A **comparison study** looks at different groups/tools/methods/categories of people and how they differ with respect to certain variables — it examines the effect of the IV on the DV **via grouping**:

- **Experimental group:** receives manipulation of the IV.
- **Baseline/control group:** no manipulation of the IV.

**Typical comparative-study structure:** Measurement of DV (baseline) → Treatment (IV) _[experimental group]_ / No treatment _[control group]_ → Measurement of DV (post) → **compare the two groups' change** to find out if the IV caused an effect in the DV.

_Worked example: teaching programming with vs. without an automated tool that gives feedback_ — experimental group gets the tool (the IV/"treatment"), control group doesn't; both are measured on the DV before and after.

### Main types of comparison studies (time-horizon axis — connects to §2's Research Onion)

- **Cross-sectional:** multiple different people/groups measured at (roughly) one point in time.
- **Longitudinal:** the same people/group measured repeatedly over time.
- **Combination of both:** overlapping cross-sectional snapshots taken at multiple time points (different groups measured at each of several time points).

**Worked example — Post-Stroke: Does music listening expedite recovery?**

- 60 stroke patients tested at baseline (neuropsychological testing, MEG & MRI, ~1 week post-stroke), then **randomly allocated to three groups (n=20 each)**: music group (standard care + self-selected music, min. 1 h/day), audio-book group (standard care + self-selected audiobooks, min. 1 h/day), and control group (standard care only). Follow-up testing at 3 months and 6 months (with some drop-outs: n=55 at 3 months, n=54 at 6 months).
- **Class discussion question: is this cross-sectional, longitudinal, or a combination?** — since the _same_ 60 (later 54) patients are followed and re-tested at three separate time points (baseline, 3 months, 6 months), this is a **longitudinal** design (with three comparison groups running in parallel).

### Types of Experiments

Four broad categories, from most to least controlled — matching the "controlled → quasi → non-experimental / field" spectrum first introduced under §5's Project Types:

**1. Controlled ("true") experiments**

- Complete control over the manipulation of the IV and control of all other relevant variables.
- Have **randomly assigned** participants to a condition (if using independent samples).
- Aim to **establish cause and effect**.
- _Example: choosing the ideal location to place an advertisement on a webpage — testing ad placement (IV) on click-through/attention (DV) with full control over layout._
- **Cons:** conducted in environments that are not natural for participants (non-contextual), so the behavior observed might not reflect what happens in real life.

**2. Quasi-experiments** ("almost" controlled)

- Do **not** have complete control over all central variables.
- **Random allocation of participants is not possible.**
- The IV in question is often something that is an **innate characteristic** (e.g., personality, gender) rather than something you can randomly assign.
- _Single-group design example:_ Students → participate in an exchange program (IV) → measure attitudes toward immigrants (DV) — there's no random assignment to "exchange program" vs. "no exchange program," so any pre-existing differences between students who chose to go and those who didn't are a threat to the conclusion.
- _True vs. quasi field experiment, side by side:_ **True field experiment** — randomly assigning different neighborhoods to receive different styles of music in public spaces, then measuring changes in social interaction (random assignment preserved, just done in the field). **Quasi field experiment** — choosing neighborhoods that _already_ differ in music exposure and comparing them (no randomization) — the neighborhoods might also differ in income, demographics, etc., which are confounds.

**3. Non-experiments ("natural" experiments)**

- For **descriptive or analytical research** (§12).
- Relies on **observation** to interpret and come to a conclusion.
- **Lacks manipulation of the IV**, typically due to **ethical considerations** (e.g., you can't ethically assign people to sleep-deprivation or violent-video-game conditions long-term).
- _Examples: epidemiological settings; comparing people who already eat organically vs. those who don't on a health assessment; observing what happens to people who already don't sleep enough and their rates of depression; a PUBG-style "does playing violent games change behavior" question that can only be studied by comparing existing players vs. non-players, not by randomly forcing people to play._

**4. Field Research (a non-experimental category with its own five sub-methods)**

- Conducted in the **"real" world / outside the laboratory**, in the **natural environment** of the people studied.
- Observes and **captures naturally occurring behavior**.
- It **may** be possible to manipulate the IV and use random allocation — on rare occasions field research can be considered a **"true" experiment** — but it's generally **not easy to minimize the effects of other variables** and control for them.
- Five sub-methods, arranged in a cycle: **Ethnography, Qualitative Interviews, Direct Observation, Participant Observation, Case Study.**
- **Observational and Descriptive** in character.

**Field Research — the four observer/participant roles:**

|Role|Description|
|---|---|
|**Complete observer (detached)**|No participation, role not revealed|
|**Observer as (known) participant**|No participation, but role is revealed to the group|
|**Complete participant (undercover/spy)**|Full participation, role not revealed|
|**Participant as (known) observer**|Full participation, and role is revealed|

**When is participant observation especially useful?**

- **Studying subcultures or groups with unique practices or beliefs:** participant observation fosters a deep, intimate understanding of the beliefs, values, and practices of a group from an **insider's perspective**; especially useful for **marginalized groups** or groups **resistant to observation**.
- **Studying complex social interactions:** immersing yourself in the group lets you observe firsthand and gain a more nuanced understanding of how interactions actually flow (rather than trying to infer it from outside).
- **Studying behaviors/practices difficult to self-report:** participants may be unwilling or unable to accurately report their own behavior; direct observation gets around this, improving data accuracy.
- _Worked examples discussed in class:_ "Scaling Classroom IT Skill Tutoring: A Case Study from India" (Joshi, Joshi, & Rangaswamy, CHI 2018) — a case study of how Ameerpet's informal IT-tuition ecosystem works; and a discussion prompt about gig-economy delivery workers (Swiggy) and fingerprint/biometric authentication — both framed as "what type of experiment/field method would this be?" exercises.

### Laboratory studies vs. field studies — trade-offs

| |**Laboratory studies**|**Field studies**|
|---|---|---|
|**Advantages**|Greater control over variables; **high internal validity**|**High ecological/external validity**; few demand characteristics|
|**Disadvantages**|Narrow definition of IV and DV; **low generalizability**; artificial; demand characteristics|Lack of control, no random allocation; **more confounds**; can be expensive and time-consuming; ethical issues (e.g., lack of informed consent)|

- **Internal validity** = confidence that the manipulated IV, not a confound, caused the observed effect in the DV (highest in tightly controlled lab experiments — see §16's glossary entry).
- **External/ecological validity** = how well findings generalize beyond the specific study setting to real life (highest in field studies).
- This is the same fundamental trade-off already illustrated by the six "heat makes people aggressive" designs in §4 — tightly controlled lab manipulations buy you causal certainty at the cost of realism, while field/naturalistic methods buy you realism at the cost of causal certainty.

### Demand characteristics

**Demand characteristics** = _"pleasing the experimenter"_ — cues in the experimental setup (the way the study is presented, the setting, the experimenter's behavior) that inadvertently signal to participants what response is "expected," causing them to (consciously or not) alter their behavior to match. This is why the "don't give hints about the right way of responding" rule under Confounding Variables above matters, and why lab settings — despite their internal-validity advantages — carry this as a specific extra risk (illustrated in class with an image referencing the Stanford Prison Experiment's guard/prisoner role-conformity).

### Comparison table: five research-design methodologies (from a summary slide)

|Feature|True Experiment|Field Experiment|Quasi-Experiment|Natural Experiment|Standard Non-Experiment|
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
|Researcher manipulates IV?|Yes (strict control)|Yes (in real world)|Yes (hands-on intervention)|No (occurs naturally)|No (observational only)|
|Environment|Artificial lab setting|Real-world setting|Real-world or clinic|Real-world setting|Real-world setting|
|How groups are formed|Strict random assignment|Strict random assignment|Pre-existing intact groups (e.g., schools, age brackets)|Forced by an outside, unpredictable event|Self-selected / pre-existing traits|
|Control over extraneous variables|High|Moderate|Moderate to Low|Low|Very Low|
|Ability to prove causation|Highest|High|Moderate|Moderate ("as-if" random)|Lowest (mostly correlational)|
|Example|Testing a drug by assigning participants to a pill or placebo group in a clinic|Testing a new teaching method by randomly assigning different classrooms to use it|Giving a new therapy program to Clinic A, using Clinic B as the control group|Studying health outcomes after a sudden city-wide cigarette ban takes effect|Surveying lifelong smokers about their daily habits and overall health|

**Key exam skill:** given any scenario, you should be able to (1) name which of these five categories it belongs to, (2) identify the IV and DV, (3) identify the setting, and (4) flag likely confounds — this is exactly the drill format used in the four "guess the experiment type" slides in class (Bus vs. Taxi Drivers/MRI; Serotonin & the brain; Saint Helena TV study; Light Therapy for depression). For instance:

- _Bus vs. Taxi Drivers (MRI comparison of brain structure by driving experience)_ → **Quasi-experiment** (driving experience/occupation is an existing trait, not randomly assigned) in a **lab/clinical setting** (MRI scanner).
- _Saint Helena Study (TV introduced to an island, behavior measured before/after)_ → **Natural experiment** (the TV introduction was a real-world event the researchers didn't control, though they did plan to measure around it) in a **field setting**.
- _Light Therapy for depression (patients randomly assigned to red/bright/soft light conditions in their own bedrooms)_ → **True or field experiment** depending on how strictly "random assignment in a real-world setting" is read — it has random assignment (true-experiment-like) but takes place in participants' own homes (field-like), making it a good discussion case for the blurry line between "true" and "field" experiments.

## 20. Operationalization

### What is operationalization?

- **op·er·a·tion·a·lize** (verb): removing ambiguity in written work and research by defining all relevant variables such that they can be **objectively measured**.
- More formally: **a process of defining the measurement of a phenomenon that is not directly measurable**, though its existence is implied by other phenomena — i.e., the **processes or steps taken to measure an abstract concept in terms of its observable, measurable characteristics**.
- This is the same concept already introduced via the Wheel of Research (§12) and the Formulating-the-Research-Problem section (§13) — Lecture 6 is essentially the deep dive on that one step.

### Conceptualization vs. Operationalization

| |**Conceptualization**|**Operationalization**|
|---|---|---|
|What it is|The process of **defining or specifying concepts**|The process by which a researcher precisely specifies **how a concept will be measured**|
|Involves|Defining/specifying what we mean when using certain terms|Developing specific research definitions that will bring about empirical observations representing those concepts in the real world|
|Main purpose|Refining and specifying **abstract concepts**|Removing vagueness and making sure concepts are **measurable**|
|Order|**First** step in the measurement process|**Second** step in the measurement process|

**Practice table (operationalize the variable):**

|The variable|The operationalized variable|
|---|---|
|Aggression from children in a playground|_(exercise — e.g., number of hitting/pushing incidents observed per 10-minute period)_|
|Problem solving ability|_(exercise — e.g., number of puzzles correctly solved within a time limit)_|
|Older adults|_(exercise — e.g., adults aged 65 and above, per a specific inclusion criterion)_|
|Stress|_(exercise — e.g., self-reported score on a validated stress scale, or cortisol level)_|

### Worked example — "People perform worse when watched" (Julie & Pete's driving conversation)

A full case study walking through the entire pipeline from an everyday claim to a testable design — ties together §5 (IV/DV), §15 (hypotheses), and §19 (experiment design) into one worked example:

**The everyday observation (a conversation transcript used as prompt):** Julie complains she drives smoothly alone but makes mistakes (crashing gears, stalling) when her friend Susie is in the car, and generalizes: _"I'm sure people perform worse when someone important's watching them."_

**Step 1 — State the hypothesis:** Hₐ = _People's performance is affected when they are being watched while driving._

**Step 2 — Identify DV and IV within the hypothesis sentence:**

- **DV** = **performance** (the thing being measured/observed).
- **IV** = **being watched** (the thing being manipulated/compared).

**Step 3 — Identify Setting, Task/Condition, Sample:**

- **Setting:** lab/controlled.
- **Task/Condition:** initially left open ("?"), then specified as a **sensorimotor task** (a wire-loop "steady-hand" style apparatus where touching the wire triggers an error) — chosen because it's a task with an objective, easily-measurable performance criterion, standing in for "driving" without needing an actual car.
- **Sample:** to be determined (raises the general question of population/sampling, a Research-Onion "Techniques & Procedures" concern from §2).
- **Comparison study or single trial?** — posed as an open design decision, connecting back to §19's Comparison Studies section: do you compare one group across two conditions (repeated measures) or two different groups (independent measures)? This is answered next.

**Step 4 — Operationalize DV and IV concretely:**

- **DV → "a measure of performance"** operationalized as **number of errors** (touching the wire) — pictured as a "NO ERRORS" icon, i.e., lower error count = better performance.
- **IV → "being watched"** operationalized as two conditions: **condition A** = an audience present (people watching, wearing 3D-glasses in the class's illustration) vs. **condition B** = an empty room/no audience.

**Step 5 — Choose Sample / design type:** This is where the **within-subjects vs. between-subjects** decision (below) gets made explicit, using the same task/audience/performance-measure setup.

### Within-subjects vs. Between-subjects design

| |**Within subjects** (repeated measures / related design)|**Between subjects** (independent measures / unrelated design)|
|---|---|---|
|Setup|The **same participants** do the sensorimotor task under **both** condition A (audience) and condition B (no audience)|**Different participants** are assigned to condition A **or** condition B, not both|
|Key problem|**Order effects** (a constant/systematic error — e.g., practice/learning or fatigue changes performance across trials regardless of condition)|Need to **match participants** across groups, and need **roughly twice as many** participants overall|
|Solution to the problem|**Counterbalancing** — alternate the order across participants (AB, BA, BA, BA, AB, …), or **complex counterbalancing (ABBA)** — each participant does A, B, B, A in that order, and their score for condition A is taken as the mean of their two A trials (same for B); also controlling for **elapsed time** between trials to counter learning/fatigue effects|**Matched-pair design** — pair participants on relevant characteristics before assigning them to different conditions. Problem: matched pairs are **not easy to find, time-consuming**, and variance may still differ between the two groups. Solution: use appropriate **statistical tests (e.g., permutation tests)** that don't assume the groups are identical in every other respect|
|Pros|**Fewer participants** needed overall; **minimizes random error** (each person acts as their own baseline)|**Minimizes learning/transfer effects across conditions** (no risk that doing condition A changes how someone does in condition B); **less time needed per participant**|
|Cons|Not suitable for some experiments; the **aim of the research becomes obvious** to participants after seeing both conditions (raising demand-characteristics risk, §19); **availability** of participants for all conditions can be limited|Needing **twice as many participants**; harder to isolate the pure effect of the IV from pre-existing individual differences between the two separate groups|
|When especially handy|Default choice when order effects can be adequately controlled|**Post-hoc evaluation/validation** — e.g., first study a group of musicians in an fMRI scanner (expensive, low-n, hard to repeat), then **separately** validate/extend the finding with a larger, cheaper between-subjects study (e.g., headphone-listening group vs. a comparison group) — using the two designs as complementary stages of the same research program rather than a single design choice|

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- The IV/DV/Controlled-variable framing here is the practical, worked-example version of the abstract definitions already given in §5.
- The Julie/Pete example is a complete, compressed walk-through of the **Wheel of Research** (§12): everyday observation → hypothesis → operationalization → data collection design — all in one scenario.
- Within-subjects vs. between-subjects is the concrete version of the "Comparison Studies" experimental-group-vs-control-group structure from §19, applied to the question of _how the same two conditions get assigned to people_ rather than just _that_ there are two conditions.
- Order effects and counterbalancing are a specific case of the **random/constant extraneous variables** distinction from §19 — an order effect is a _constant_ (systematic) source of error unless counterbalancing is used to spread it out and neutralize it.

## 21. Additional Glossary Terms (Lectures 6 & 7)

- **Independent variable (IV) / Dependent variable (DV) / Controlled variable:** what you change / what you observe / what you keep the same — the three components identified at the start of any experiment design.
- **Extraneous variable:** an undesirable variable, other than the IV, that influences the outcome of an experiment; subtypes are random, constant, and confounding.
- **Random variable (as extraneous-variable type):** creates unpredictable, unsystematic error in measurement.
- **Constant variable (as extraneous-variable type):** a source of constant/systematic error causing measurements to deviate consistently from their true value.
- **Confounding variable:** an extraneous variable that changes systematically along with the IV, providing an alternative explanation for the results; can cause false positives, false negatives, or misattributed causal components.
- **Comparison study:** examines the effect of an IV on a DV by comparing an experimental group (receives the IV manipulation) against a baseline/control group (does not).
- **Cross-sectional / longitudinal / combination design:** comparison studies structured as one-time snapshots across groups, repeated measurement of the same group over time, or a mix of both.
- **True (controlled) experiment:** complete control over IV manipulation and other variables, random assignment where applicable, aims to establish cause and effect.
- **Quasi-experiment:** lacks complete control over central variables and random allocation; the IV is often an innate/pre-existing characteristic (e.g., personality, gender, occupation).
- **Non-experiment ("natural" experiment):** descriptive/analytical research relying on observation, with no manipulation of the IV, typically for ethical reasons.
- **Field research:** research conducted in participants' natural environment rather than a lab; includes ethnography, qualitative interviews, direct observation, participant observation, and case study.
- **Participant observation roles:** complete observer (detached, role hidden), observer as known participant (role revealed, no participation), complete participant (undercover), participant as known observer (role revealed, participates).
- **Internal validity:** confidence that the IV, not a confound, caused the effect in the DV; typically highest in controlled lab experiments.
- **External/ecological validity:** how well findings generalize beyond the specific study sample/setting; typically highest in field studies.
- **Demand characteristics:** cues in an experimental setting that lead participants to guess the study's purpose and adjust their behavior to "please the experimenter."
- **Operationalization:** the process of defining how an abstract, not-directly-measurable phenomenon will be measured in terms of observable, measurable characteristics; the second step in the measurement process, after conceptualization.
- **Conceptualization:** the first step in the measurement process — defining or specifying what a concept/term means, before deciding how to measure it.
- **Within-subjects (repeated measures / related) design:** the same participants experience all conditions; main risk is order effects, addressed via counterbalancing.
- **Between-subjects (independent measures / unrelated) design:** different participants are assigned to different conditions; main cost is needing more participants and matching them, addressed via matched-pair designs or appropriate statistical tests.
- **Counterbalancing / complex counterbalancing (ABBA):** techniques for spreading or neutralizing order effects across conditions in a within-subjects design.
- **Matched-pair design:** pairing participants on relevant traits before assigning them to different between-subjects conditions, to reduce the confound of pre-existing individual differences.
