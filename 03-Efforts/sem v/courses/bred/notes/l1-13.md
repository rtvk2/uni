## 1. What is Behavioral Research?

Behavioral research is an **umbrella term** for research in the social and behavioral sciences — health policy, social work, cognitive science, HCI, management, etc. — that deals with human behavior.

Three ways to define it (know all three, they overlap):
1. **A way to examine and understand** individual and social behavior through *measurement and interpretation*.
2. **The study of observable behavior and inferred mental processes.** Goal: discover how people perceive their world, think, feel, change over time, learn, make decisions, and interact with others. Includes perception, decision-making, memory, emotion, social behavior.
3. Uses **both quantitative and qualitative methods** to measure and understand human behavior.

**Interdisciplinary relevance:** Psychology, Education, Marketing, Public Health, Neuroscience, and more.

Key working principle: researchers **collect data and systematically compare treatment and control conditions** on human behavior/choice — though a control condition is *not always possible* (e.g., you can't randomly assign people to be sleep-deprived long-term, or to have a personality trait).

**Adaptive nature:** behavioral research can work with whatever database/prior research exists — written notes, recorded interviews, or "big data."

### Why does this matter? (Skills gained)
From the textbook (Ch.1), the course builds two kinds of skill:
- **Skills as a researcher** — how to design, run, and analyze a study yourself.
- **Skills as a consumer of research** — how to critically evaluate claims you encounter (e.g., a magazine survey, a news headline "Study shows X causes Y"). Key critical questions: *Who* was studied? *How* were they sampled? *How many*? What fraction responded? Could the answers to these change the result?

### Goals of Science (textbook, Ch.3 — underpins "why do behavioral research")
Most scientists pursue three goals, and they build on each other:
1. **Understanding (description → explanation):** first just accurately *describe* what's happening (the "what"); as the description gets more complete — more factors/conditions identified — it becomes an *explanation* (the "why").
2. **Prediction:** if we understand something, we should be able to predict when/whether it will occur. Prediction doesn't always require full understanding, though (e.g., we can predict that some depressed patients improve after a treatment without fully knowing the underlying mechanism).
3. **Control:** only possible when the researcher can actually manipulate the phenomenon (this is why *experiments* — which manipulate variables — are considered the strongest design for establishing cause-effect, vs. purely observational sciences like astronomy, which can predict but never control).

### Two assumptions all scientists make (Ch.3)
1. **Determinism:** behavior is lawful/orderly — it's a function of prior (antecedent) events, not random or capricious.
2. **Discoverability:** this lawfulness can, at least in principle, be discovered through research.
- These are treated as *useful working assumptions*, not proven facts — science "sticks by the rules of the game" as long as they keep producing understanding/prediction/control, and revises them (a "paradigm shift," e.g., Ptolemy's earth-centered model being replaced by Copernicus's sun-centered one) when they stop being useful.

### Basic vs. Applied Research (Ch.1/Ch.3 — relevant to how project topics were framed in class)
- **Basic research:** aimed at understanding a phenomenon for its own sake, with no immediate practical goal in mind (not "mission-oriented").
- **Applied research:** aimed at solving a specific, practical, real-world problem.
- Many of the in-class project examples (ad-placement & attention, FinTech adoption, music bias) are **applied** — they target a specific real-world question — while something like "can color palette affect emotional valence" leans more toward **basic** research into a general psychological mechanism. Note: basic research often ends up being applied years later (the textbook's examples: X-rays, vaccines, computer chips all emerged from basic research with no planned application) — so the basic/applied line isn't a judgment of importance.

## 3. Experiment Design as a Cycle (BRSM vs BRED)

Two overlapping cycles were shown:

**BRSM (outer loop):** research question → **report** → **evaluation** → data collection & analysis → back to research question.

**BRED (inner loop, shares the "research question" and "data collection & analysis" nodes):** research question → **problem definition** → **experimental design** → data collection & analysis.

Interpretation: research is **iterative**, not linear — you don't just design once and stop; evaluation of results can send you back to refine the question, and problem definition happens between the question and the actual experimental design.

### Finding Data — the basic requirements
Before designing anything, you need to ask the journalistic questions about your topic and your data:
**Who? What? Where? When? Why? How?**

And you must assess **data quality** — where is the data coming from, is it reliable, is it representative, is it recent/relevant?

## 4. Core Logic of Science: Support vs. Proof

This is one of the most testable conceptual points in the slides.

> **"Scientific research does not prove theories true."**

Research only ever **supports or contradicts** a theory — it never proves it, because there is always an alternative explanation. Correct phrasing to use in scientific writing:
- "*All the evidence so far points towards…*"
- "*The evidence is consistent with…*"

### Worked example: The broccoli allergy
A younger brother gets a rash every Monday. He eats broccoli every Sunday (family dinner). You remove broccoli; the rash doesn't return for 4 weeks.

**Question posed: Has this "proved" broccoli was the problem?**
**Answer: No.**
- If the rash disappears → this is **support for, not proof of**, the broccoli-allergy theory. Alternative explanation: it could have been an herb Gran always cooks alongside the broccoli.
- If the rash *remains* → this gives a more definite result that appears to **rule out** the broccoli theory — though even here there's an outside chance he's allergic to broccoli *and* something else served that day.
- **Key method insight:** by removing one item at a time while holding everything else constant, you isolate the cause — this is essentially describing **experimental control** (manipulate one variable, hold others constant).

### Worked example: Ayurveda and COVID-19 (critical evaluation exercise)
A study gave policemen Ayurvedic medicine, then measured COVID-19 incidence over 6 months and compared it to the general population. Infection rate was lower in the policemen group → researchers concluded Ayurveda prevents/reduces COVID-19 risk.

**How to critique this (general framework, applies broadly to any "should I trust this conclusion" question):**
- **No control/comparability:** Policemen ≠ general population on many other variables (age, fitness, occupational exposure patterns, healthcare access, mask/PPE use, testing rates, baseline health) — any of these, not the medicine, could explain the difference.
- **No random assignment / no proper control group** drawn from the same population and treated identically except for the intervention.
- **Confounds are not ruled out** — this is a correlational/quasi observation, not a controlled experiment, so causal claims ("Ayurveda prevents COVID") are not warranted.
- **What would strengthen the claim:** a randomized controlled trial with a comparable control group (e.g., other policemen not given the medicine), blinding, matched demographics, and controlling for exposure/testing differences.
- This exercise is testing the same "support vs. proof" and "confound" concepts as the broccoli example, just in a real-world/quasi-experimental dress.

### Worked example: Does eating only fish increase IQ?
Shown as a template for the full research cycle:
Research question → **H1** (alternative hypothesis) and **H0** (null hypothesis) → **Experiment** → Result: "no significant difference between groups" → loop back to H1/H0 → **Conclusion: only eating fish does not increase IQ.**
This illustrates the standard **hypothesis-testing pipeline**: pose question → derive testable hypotheses → run experiment → statistical result → conclusion (which itself is only ever "supported" or "not supported," never "proven").

### Worked example: "Heat makes people aggressive" — operationalizing one theory many ways
The class was shown ~6 *different* possible experimental designs to test the *same* theory, illustrating that one theory can be operationalized many ways, each with different trade-offs (control vs realism, causal power vs ethics/feasibility):
1. Have people solve problems in a hot room, then a cold room; measure blood pressure. *(within-subjects, physiological measure)*
2. One group solves problems in a hot room, another in a cool room; have them tear cardboard afterward and observationally rate aggression. *(between-subjects, behavioral measure)*
3. Observe horn-honking by drivers on hot vs. cold days. *(naturalistic observation, field, no manipulation)*
4. Put people in a hot or cold room, then interview using an aggression scale. *(self-report)*
5. Approach people on hot/cold days and administer an aggression scale (if they agree). *(field survey)*
6. Check public crime records for aggression-related crime in hot vs. cold seasons, same city. *(archival research)*

**Takeaway:** these span the spectrum from tightly **controlled lab experiments** (1, 2) to **quasi/field/naturalistic** methods (3, 5) to **archival research** (6) — same research question, very different designs, each with different strengths for internal validity (causal certainty) vs. external validity (real-world generalizability).

### Why science can only ever "support," never "prove" — the deeper reason (confirmation bias & falsifiability)
This grounds the slide's "scientific research does not prove theories true" line in the actual textbook mechanism, and is a natural follow-up exam angle.

- **Confirmation bias:** the tendency to keep testing/collecting evidence that *could* confirm your hypothesis, rather than actively trying to rule it out. Classic illustration: given the sequence "2, 4, 6" and told it fits a hidden rule, most people guess "increasing by 2" and only ever test *other* increasing-by-2 sequences (which keep getting confirmed) — never realizing the actual rule was simply "any three ascending numbers." You can generate confirming evidence for a wrong theory *forever*.
- **The fix — seek disconfirmation (falsification):** the fastest way to test a hypothesis is to try to *break* it, not just support it. This is why a good study design tries to rule out alternative explanations (i.e., it's the same logic as isolating the broccoli/herb confound, or asking what else besides Ayurveda could explain the policemen's lower infection rate).
- **Consequence:** because you can never test *every* possible alternative explanation, and because confirming instances never logically rule out all other theories, a theory is only ever **"supported/confirmed"** or **"unsupported/disconfirmed" — never proven true or false with certainty.** This is exactly the "support vs. proof" language from the slides, just with the underlying reason spelled out.

### Four ways of knowing (where "scientific" evidence sits relative to other sources)
Useful for critically evaluating any claim (e.g., the Ayurveda study, or an ad claiming "scientifically proven"):
1. **Authority** — believing something because an expert/text/institution said so (risk: even experts/institutions can be wrong or biased, e.g., politically-motivated pseudoscience).
2. **Personal experience** — belief from your own observations (risk: overgeneralizing from a small, unrepresentative sample of your own life).
3. **Rationalism** — belief from logic/reasoning alone, without testing it empirically (risk: a logically valid conclusion can still be false if its starting premise was never empirically checked).
4. **Empiricism** — belief grounded in systematic, repeatable, testable observation. This is the backbone of the scientific method and what distinguishes science from non-science.
- **Practical test for "is this scientific?":** does the claim have an **empirical referent** (something observable/measurable, directly or indirectly)? Is it **repeatable** (can another researcher verify it elsewhere)? Is the field **self-correcting** (does it revise theories when new evidence contradicts them)? Claims that fail these (e.g., unfalsifiable claims) aren't scientific, regardless of how "sciency" they sound.
- Note: 
	- **Validity** is about structure. If the premises are true, the conclusion cannot be false.
	- **Truth** is about reality. It checks if the premises match the real world.
	- **Soundness** means the argument is valid _and_ the premises are true in fact.

### Observation vs. Inference (Ch.3 — directly underlies the Ayurveda/broccoli-style critique questions)
Two distinct activities happen in any study, and mixing them up is the root of most bad conclusions:
- **Observation:** the raw, accurate recording of what happened (e.g., "policemen who took Ayurvedic medicine had a lower COVID infection rate over 6 months").
- **Inference:** the *interpretation* built on top of the observation (e.g., "therefore Ayurveda prevents COVID"). Inference includes explaining the data, guessing at underlying causes, and building new concepts from it.
- **Key point: observations can be perfectly accurate and repeatable while the inference drawn from them is still wrong**, because the same observation can have multiple possible explanations. Textbook examples:
  - A cockroach that stops responding to "Jump!" after having all its legs removed is observed accurately — but the inference "losing all legs makes a cockroach deaf" is absurd (it can no longer *feel* the vibration cue, it isn't "deaf").
  - A traveler accurately observes that older people in an ethnic neighborhood tend to have stronger accents, but wrongly infers "people develop accents as they age" (rather than: older residents simply immigrated earlier, before English became as dominant).
- **Direct application:** this is exactly the flaw in the Ayurveda/COVID example — the *observation* (lower infection rate in the treated group) may be perfectly real, but the *inference* (Ayurveda caused it) ignores other explanations (occupational differences, demographics, exposure), i.e., confounds. Any time a slide/question asks you to "critique a conclusion," check whether the inference is the *only* explanation consistent with the observation, or just *one* of several.

### Molecular-to-Molar Continuum (Ch.3 — grounds "interdisciplinary relevance" from §1)
Different fields study behavior at different "grain sizes," and none is more "correct" than another — they're complementary:
**Physics (atoms/subatomic) → Chemistry (molecules) → Physiology (body systems) → Psychology (the individual organism's behavior) → Sociology (group behavior) → Ethnology (culture).**
- "Molecular" = smaller/more reductionist unit of analysis; "molar" = larger/more holistic unit. These are *relative* terms — psychology is molecular compared to sociology, but molar compared to chemistry.
- This directly explains why behavioral research slides describe the field as **interdisciplinary** (Psychology, Education, Marketing, Public Health, Neuroscience, HCI, etc.) — each discipline sits at a different point on this continuum, but they all inform each other when studying human behavior.

### Formulating hypotheses & variables (grounds the "operationalize" idea, CO-4)
- A **hypothesis** is an educated, logically-derived prediction (not just "what I think should happen") — good hypotheses have (a) a clear rationale/reasoning for the prediction, (b) an **if–then** structure, (c) a clear statement of the relationship between the variables involved. This is the more detailed version of the H1/H0 boxes in the fish/IQ example.
- **Independent variable (IV):** the condition manipulated/controlled by the researcher (the presumed "cause"). Can be **qualitative** (differs in *kind*, e.g., type of psychotherapy) or **quantitative** (differs in *amount*, e.g., dosage level).
- **Dependent variable (DV):** the behavior/outcome measured, whose changes presumably *depend on* the IV (the presumed "effect"). If a systematic IV→DV relationship is found, that's an empirical/causal (or "functional") relationship.
- This is the concrete mechanism behind "operationalizing" a research question (CO-4): you turn a vague idea ("does heat make people aggressive?") into a specific IV (room temperature: hot vs. cold) and a specific, measurable DV (cardboard-tearing behavior, self-reported aggression score, horn-honking frequency, crime records) — exactly what the six heat-aggression designs in §4 each did differently.

## 6. Research Philosophy (top layer of the Onion)

Four stances on **what counts as reality and how we can know it**, each with characteristic methods:

### Positivism
- Emphasizes **objective, observable facts**: *"I don't want opinions, give me the facts!"*
- Relies heavily on **quantitative methods**.
- Believes in a **single, measurable reality**, studied via scientific observation and experimentation.
- Common in natural sciences, adapted for social science.
- Aligned with the **hypothetico-deductive model**: verify *a priori* hypotheses via experimentation, operationalizing variables/measures; results inform/advance science.
- Focus: identifying **explanatory associations or causal relationships** via quantitative approaches.

### Interpretivism
- Emphasizes the **subjective nature of reality**; understanding social phenomena through participants' own perspectives.
- Associated with **qualitative methods**; prevalent in social sciences.
- Reality is **socially constructed**; understood through in-depth exploration of human experience and meaning.
- Methods: interviews, focus groups, participant observation — rich qualitative data.
- The researcher **co-constructs knowledge** and emphasizes context.

### Pragmatism
- Focuses on **practical outcomes and problem-solving**; "**Whatever works**."
- Concerned with finding **workable solutions** to research questions; useful for applied research with real-world implications.
- Often employs **mixed methods** (quant + qual together).
- Allows drawing from multiple philosophies/methods flexibly, choosing whatever best answers the question at hand.

### Realism
- Seeks to **bridge positivism and interpretivism**.
- Acknowledges an **objective reality** exists, while recognizing our *understanding* of it is shaped by social/historical context.
- Believes in a **stratified reality**: the **empirical** (what we can observe), the **actual** (events that occur, whether observed or not), and the **real** (underlying causal mechanisms)
- Uses a mix of quantitative and qualitative methods to explore both observable phenomena and the deeper structures behind them.
- Useful for understanding complex social systems; informs both theory and practical intervention.

### Worked example — Generative AI & academic assignments (recurring example across all 4 philosophies)
| Topic             | Positivist framing (quant, "what/is there an effect")         | Interpretivist framing (qual, "how do people experience it")                                 |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Learning outcomes | Does GenAI use improve assignment grades among undergrads?    | How do undergrads perceive GenAI's influence on their learning while completing assignments? |
| Critical thinking | Is AI-use frequency associated with critical thinking skills? | How do students describe AI's role in shaping their thinking/problem-solving?                |
| Time management   | Does AI use reduce time required to complete assignments?     | How do students experience the ways AI changes their workflow/time management?               |

- **Positivist question:** "Does the frequency of generative AI use improve students' assignment grades?" → measure AI usage frequency + assignment grades → statistical analysis.
- **Interpretivist question:** "How do students experience the use of generative AI while completing academic assignments?" → interviews, reflective journals → thematic analysis.
- **Pragmatist question:** "How does the use of generative AI influence students' learning, assignment quality, and perceptions of learning, and how can universities design effective policies for its use?" — combines *what is happening* (quant: survey 500 students, measure AI usage, compare grades), *why is it happening* (qual: interview 25 students, explore how they actually use AI, identify challenges/benefits), and *what should be done* (practical application/policy).

### Worked example — music streaming research questions, mapped to philosophy
- *"What is the correlation between listening frequency and user demographics?"* → **Positivism** (quantitative, correlational).
- *"How do users construct identity through their playlists?"* → **Interpretivism** (meaning-making, qualitative).
- *"How can listening data and interviews improve recommendation algorithms?"* → **Pragmatism** (practical/applied, mixed methods).
- *"What mechanisms influence music consumption patterns on streaming platforms?"* → **Realism** (underlying causal mechanisms, stratified reality).

**How to recognize philosophy from a question (exam-style skill):** look at the verb/goal.
- "Does X affect/predict/improve Y?" / correlation, comparison of means → **Positivism**.
- "How do people experience/perceive/construct meaning around X?" → **Interpretivism**.
- "How can we use this to solve/design/improve a real system?" → **Pragmatism**.
- "What underlying mechanism explains/produces X?" (bridging observable + deeper structure) → **Realism**.

## 7. Research Approach (second layer of the Onion): Induction, Deduction, Abduction

- **Induction:** Research **starts with observation**, moves to description/analysis of data, in order to **form a theory**. Used when developing a new theory, or in fields with less prior research.
  - *Example:* "What behavioral characteristics define human interaction in cooperative, partially observable communication settings? Can stable, role-specific playing styles be identified from player interactions?"
  - *Example:* "What different styles of binge-watching can be identified from viewing histories?"
- **Deduction:** Research **starts with an existing theory**, then raises a question/hypothesis, collects data to **confirm or reject the hypothesis**. Applied for **testing existing theory**.
  - *Example:* "Does loneliness predict binge-watching frequency?"
  - *Example:* "Does perceived recommendation quality increase binge-watching?"
- **Abduction:** Starts from **observation of an empirical/surprising phenomenon**, then research seeks the **best-guess/most likely explanation** based on available evidence. Moves *between* induction and deduction.
  - *Example:* "Why do Netflix users frequently ignore recommendations that they consider highly accurate?" (a surprising fact in need of the best explanation)

**Quick way to tell them apart:** Deduction = *theory → hypothesis → test*. Induction = *data/observation → theory*. Abduction = *surprising observation → best available explanation* (neither strictly proving nor purely theory-building).

## 8. Research Strategies & Choices (third/fourth layers of the Onion)

### Strategies
- **Survey:** Collects data from many participants using questionnaires or structured interviews.
- **Case Study:** In-depth investigation of a single case, organization, or event (classic historical example referenced: Phineas Gage, the man with the tamping-iron brain injury).
- **Archival Research:** Analyzes existing records, documents, or historical data to answer research questions.
- **Ethnography:** Studies people in their natural environment to understand their culture and behaviors (example image: a large religious gathering — studying group behavior in situ).
- **Grounded Theory:** Generation of hypotheses and theories **from** data (bottom-up). Cyclical/iterative process: **Collect data → Analyze data (code data, write memos, create concept maps) → Theoretical Sampling → Theory**, looping back to collect more data as needed.
  - *Example study:* Weatherall (2000), *"A grounded theory analysis of older adults and information technology,"* Educational Gerontology, 26(4), 371–386. Six older-adult participants interviewed about their experiences/opinions on computer technology; **no hypothesis was tested up front** — themes emerged from the analysis. Findings: participants recognized IT's importance in modern life (motivating exploration); positive attitudes developed/reinforced through direct experience and personal ownership of technology.
- **Experiment:** Tests cause-and-effect relationships by **manipulating variables** under **controlled conditions**.
- **Action Research:** A cyclical, practitioner-driven process (example: an art teacher). Cycle: **Recognize/wonder about a problem in the classroom → Think about possible reasons → Come up with solutions to try → Collect and analyze data → Analyze the solution** → (loop back).

### Research Choices — Mono/Multi/Mixed method design
- **Mono-method:** Uses a **single method** of data collection or analysis to study a phenomenon.
- **Multi-method:** Uses **multiple methods** to study a phenomenon **from different perspectives** — but all methods are from the *same* paradigm (i.e., QUAL + QUAL, or QUANT + QUANT).
- **Mixed-method:** **Combines quantitative and qualitative** methods (QUANT + QUAL) to gain both numerical and in-depth understanding of behavior.

**Distinction to remember:** Multimethod = same type combined (Qual+Qual or Quant+Quant). Mixed methods = different types combined (Quant+Qual).

## 9. Qualitative vs. Quantitative Research

### Qualitative
- Concerned with the **subjective assessment** of qualitative phenomena: attitudes, opinions, behavior.
- Can be a function of the **researcher's insights and impressions**.
- Data is **words, ideas, interviews** — subjective, observes behavior; "experience supports outcome."
- Cannot always be reduced to numbers, though it *can sometimes* be analyzed statistically.
	- Purpose: answer **"Why?"** questions. Data type: observation/symbol/word. Approach: observe and interpret. Analysis: grouping of common data / non-statistical.

### Quantitative
- Applicable to phenomena expressible **in terms of quantity**.
- Requires an **operational definition**: research question → specific definition of the measured variables → generate data → analyze formally/rigorously.
- Data is **numbers, facts** — objective; "numbers support outcome."
- Purpose: answer **"How many/how much?"** Data type: number/statistical result. Approach: measure and test. Analysis: statistical analysis.
- Three quantitative sub-approaches:
  - **Inferential (≈ induction):** form a database from which to infer characteristics or relationships of a population.
  - **Experimental (≈ deduction):** variables are manipulated to observe their effect on other variables.
  - **Simulation:** build mathematical or computational models to study systems.

### Choosing qualitative vs. quantitative (or both)
Framed as an example question: *"Should I select a qualitative or quantitative approach, or both?"* — e.g., for understanding **why users interact with an interface in certain ways** (this "why" framing signals qualitative), versus reading raw sensor/instrument values like an air-quality monitor's PM2.5/AQI/HCHO/TVOC readings (this framing signals quantitative).

**Different approaches complement each other** — three possible orderings shown:
1. Qualitative → Quantitative → Results (explore first, then measure/confirm)
2. Quantitative → Qualitative → Results (measure first, then explain the "why" behind the numbers)
3. Qualitative + Quantitative → Results (in parallel/combined)

**Mixed Methods = Qualitative Research + Quantitative Research.**

The three-camp cartoon (useful mnemonic): 
- "**Qualitative methods are best**" camp → associated with **constructivism, inductive logic, exploratory research**.
- "**The research question drives everything**" (middle, mixed-methods) camp → associated with **pragmatism, abductive logic, QUAL and QUANT analysis**.
- "**Quantitative methods are best**" camp → associated with **postpositivism, deductive logic, confirmatory research**.

This maps back cleanly onto §6–7: Qualitative↔Interpretivism↔Induction; Quantitative↔Positivism↔Deduction; Mixed↔Pragmatism↔Abduction.

## 10. How the Layers Connect (synthesis / likely exam framing)

A single research question can be approached at every level of the onion simultaneously — you should be able to state, for any given research question:
1. **Philosophy** it reflects (positivist / interpretivist / pragmatist / realist)
2. **Approach** it reflects (inductive / deductive / abductive)
3. **Strategy** best suited to it (survey / experiment / case study / ethnography / archival / grounded theory / action research)
4. **Choice** of method (mono / multi / mixed)
5. **Data type** implied (qualitative / quantitative / both) and **why**

**Worked template (from the AI-in-education example):**
- "Does GenAI use improve grades?" → Positivism → Deduction (testing a specific effect) → Survey/quasi-experiment → Mono-method (quant) → Quantitative (grades, usage frequency; statistical analysis).
- "How do students experience GenAI while completing assignments?" → Interpretivism → Induction (theory-building from lived experience) → Interviews/ethnographic → Mono-method (qual) → Qualitative (interviews, reflective journals; thematic analysis).
- "How does GenAI influence learning/quality/perception, and how should universities design policy?" → Pragmatism → Abduction (moving between the two to find the most useful/complete answer) → Mixed strategy (survey + interviews) → **Mixed-method** → Both quantitative (survey of 500, usage vs. grades) and qualitative (interview 25 students on challenges/benefits) feeding into a practical/policy recommendation.

## 11. Quick-Reference Glossary

- **Behavioral research:** interdisciplinary study of observable behavior and inferred mental processes, using quantitative and/or qualitative methods.
- **Operationalization:** defining how to measure a phenomenon that isn't directly measurable.
- **H1 / H0:** alternative / null hypothesis.
- **Research Onion:** layered model — Philosophy → Approach → Strategy → Choices → Time Horizon → Techniques & Procedures.
- **Positivism:** objective single reality, quantitative, hypothesis-testing.
- **Interpretivism:** subjective/socially-constructed reality, qualitative, meaning-focused.
- **Pragmatism:** practical problem-solving, mixed methods, "whatever works."
- **Realism:** objective reality but socially-shaped understanding; stratified (empirical/actual/real); mixed methods.
- **Induction:** observation → theory.
- **Deduction:** theory → hypothesis → test.
- **Abduction:** surprising observation → best available explanation.
- **Mono-method:** one method. **Multi-method:** multiple methods, same paradigm (all qual or all quant). **Mixed-method:** quant + qual combined.
- **Grounded theory:** theory emerges from iterative coding of data (no prior hypothesis).
- **Action research:** cyclical practitioner-led problem-solving (recognize → think → solve → collect/analyze → repeat).
- **Ethnography:** studying people in their natural setting.
- **Archival research:** using existing records/historical data.
- **Cross-sectional vs. longitudinal:** one-time snapshot vs. data over time.
- **Internal validity:** confidence that the manipulated variable, not a confound, caused the effect (highest in controlled experiments).
- **External validity / generalizability:** how well findings extend beyond the specific study sample/setting.
- **Confound / extraneous variable:** an uncontrolled variable that offers an alternative explanation for results (e.g., the herbs in the broccoli example; demographic/exposure differences in the Ayurveda example).
- **Science does not prove theories true** — it only provides evidence that supports or contradicts them.
- **Confirmation bias:** favoring evidence that confirms a hypothesis over actively seeking evidence that could disconfirm it.
- **Falsifiability:** a theory must, in principle, be testable/disprovable by evidence to count as scientific; the fastest way to test a hypothesis is to try to break it, not just support it.
- **Empirical referent:** something observable/measurable (directly or indirectly) — a requirement for a question to be studied scientifically.
- **Four ways of knowing:** authority, personal experience, rationalism, empiricism — science relies mainly on the last.
- **Independent variable (IV):** the condition manipulated by the researcher (presumed cause); qualitative (differs in kind) or quantitative (differs in amount).
- **Dependent variable (DV):** the measured behavior/outcome, presumed to depend on the IV (presumed effect).
- **Hypothesis:** a specific, logically-derived, testable prediction, ideally stated in if–then form with a clear rationale.
- **Goals of science:** understanding (description → explanation), prediction, control (control only possible when the phenomenon can be manipulated).
- **Determinism:** the assumption that behavior is lawful/orderly, i.e., a function of prior events, not random.
- **Basic vs. applied research:** basic = understanding for its own sake, no immediate practical goal; applied = aimed at a specific real-world problem. Basic research often becomes applied later.
- **Observation vs. inference:** observation = accurate raw recording of what happened; inference = the interpretation/explanation built on top. Observations can be accurate while the inference drawn from them is still wrong (confounds/alternative explanations).
- **Molecular-to-molar continuum:** different disciplines (physics → chemistry → physiology → psychology → sociology → ethnology) study behavior at different, complementary grain sizes.

## 12. Research Types: How a Study Can Be Classified

Three independent dichotomies used to classify any research question. A single study can be described on all three axes at once — they are separate spectrums, not alternatives to the Research Onion of §6–9.

### Descriptive vs. Analytical

- **Descriptive:** describes the state of affairs as it currently exists — fact-finding. Helps identify patterns and generate hypotheses for later study. The researcher has **no control over variables**; can only report what is/was happening. **Correlational, not causal** — doesn't establish cause–effect, so follow-up experimental/quasi-experimental work may be needed. Common in social sciences and business studies. _Example: tracking Xiaomi's rising market share vs. Samsung in India over time — a chart of "what happened."_
- **Analytical:** the researcher critically evaluates already-available material/data by analyzing facts and information. Focused on explaining _why_, not just reporting _what_.
- **Memorable pairing (epidemiology):** Descriptive = **"What"** (who/when/where does something occur; generates hypotheses; smaller/less complex; uses individuals or a group; case reports, case series, incidence counts). Analytical = **"Why"** (measures the association between an exposure and an outcome using individual-level data; tests hypotheses about determinants/risk factors; larger/more complex; uses comparison groups; observational + experimental studies).

### Applied vs. Fundamental

- **Applied:** aimed at finding a solution to an **immediate problem** facing a society, industry, business, or medical community. _Example: an AI-for-social-good research center pairing AI methods with a concrete social problem; a project using social-network analysis to identify influential homeless youth so HIV-prevention information spreads through their peer network._
- **Fundamental** (a.k.a. **"Pure"/"Basic" research**, same idea as §1's Basic vs. Applied Research): _"gathering knowledge for the sake of knowledge."_ Concerned with generalizations and formulating theory with a broad base of potential application — now or later — adding to the existing organized body of scientific knowledge. _Example: pure mathematics._
- **Mnemonic pairing shown in class:** music neuroscience (how the brain processes music) = fundamental; music therapy (using music to treat patients) = applied.

### Conceptual vs. Empirical

- **Conceptual:** related to abstract idea(s) or theory. Systematically developing new ideas, analyzing abstract theories, or reinterpreting existing principles through structured scholarly thinking. Associated with philosophers. _Example figure used in class: Aristotle, "developing new concepts or theories."_
- **Empirical:** relies on **experimentation**; data-based research; can reach conclusions without a specific need for pre-existing theory. Associated with experimenters/inventors ("Edison," testing to form new theories or confirm old ones). _Example figure: profiling/benchmarking C++ code — you run it and observe what actually happens, rather than reasoning about it abstractly._
- **Class discussion prompt:** Newton sitting under the apple tree — is discovering gravity from watching an apple fall conceptual or empirical? The intended takeaway is that it's **both**: an empirical observation (the apple falling) triggers conceptual theorizing (universal gravitation). Real research usually blends both ends of the axis rather than sitting purely at one pole.

### Why these classifications matter for research design

| Classification | Main question                           | Consequence for design          |
| -------------- | --------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| Descriptive    | What is happening?                      | Describe patterns               |
| Analytical     | What explains/relates to what?          | Test relationships/mechanisms   |
| Fundamental    | Why does this matter theoretically?     | Build knowledge/theory          |
| Applied        | What problem can this address?          | Develop solutions/interventions |
| Conceptual     | What can we reason about theoretically? | Develop models/frameworks       |
| Empirical      | What does evidence show?                | Collect/analyze observations    |

These three axes cut across the Research Onion of §6–9: e.g. Positivism tends to pair with Analytical + Empirical + Deductive work; Interpretivism tends to pair with Descriptive-of-meaning + Conceptual-leaning + Inductive work; Applied/Fundamental cuts across both philosophies (you can do applied-positivist or applied-interpretivist work equally).

### The Wheel of Research

A circular (cyclical, not linear) model of how a full research project moves through stages, built up piece-by-piece across several slides.

**Top half (clockwise from top): theory, ideas → models → concepts → hypotheses → operationalization → data collection.** **Bottom half (continuing clockwise): data collection → descriptions, typologies → data analysis → explanation → generalization → assessing reliability → practical implications** → back up to theory, ideas.

Two labelled entry points onto the same circle:

- **Typical starting point for quantitative research** = enters at the **top** ("theory") — you start from an existing theory, derive hypotheses, then collect data to test them. This is the **deductive** route (theory → data), matching §7.
- **Typical starting point for qualitative research** = enters at the **bottom** ("empirical data") — you start by collecting/observing data and build toward theory. This is the **inductive** route (data → theory), matching §7.

A more detailed version of the same wheel is just this circle re-drawn as an explicit loop of concrete steps: **research question → problem definition → experimental design → data collection and analysis → evaluation → report** → back to research question — the same BRSM/BRED cycle already introduced in §3.

The wheel is also labelled with the three dichotomies above sitting around its outside: **Applied / Fundamental** and **Conceptual / Empirical** label the top half (the theory-facing side); **Analytical / Descriptive** label the bottom half (the data-facing side) — visually reinforcing that fundamental/conceptual work lives closer to "theory, ideas," while descriptive/analytical work lives closer to "data analysis, descriptions."

**Key takeaway:** research is a _cycle_, not a one-way street — practical implications and reliability-assessment at the bottom feed back up into refining theory and ideas at the top, matching the "research is iterative" point already made about BRSM/BRED in §3.

## 13. Beginning of Research, Part II: Active Reading and the Literature Review

(Extends §2's "Research Onion — where do you start?" with the concrete first-weeks workflow, from Lecture 4.)

### Becoming an active (critical) reader/listener

| Non-critical reading | Critical reading      |
| -------------------- | --------------------- |
| passive              | active                |
| just the facts       | asks _what, how, why_ |
| gullible             | skeptical             |
| reactive             | purposeful            |

When reading or listening to any piece of technical work, ask:

- **Where** did the author seem to draw the ideas from?
- **What** exactly was accomplished by this piece of work?
- **How** does it relate to other work in the field?
- **What** would be a reasonable next step to build on this work?
- **What** ideas from related fields might be brought to bear on this subject?

### Turning reading into a literature review (the practical habit)

- **Log** your technical reading/listening as you go — notes on what you read, when, and answers to the questions above.
- This log **ends up being your literature review** for your study/project/thesis — far easier to write from an ongoing log than to reconstruct from memory later.
- Logging also makes **citing easier**, and a **periodic review** of the log helps ideas "begin to fit together" as patterns across papers become visible.
- **Repeat this after a few months** — re-reading your own older notes with fresh eyes often surfaces connections you missed the first time.
- **Myth to avoid:** _"Finish literature review and then start [research]."_ In practice, literature review and research design/execution run in parallel and feed each other — reading doesn't stop once you start experimenting.
- Staying an "active" reader/listener throughout the project also makes it easier to generate **alternative topics/directions** if the original plan needs to change.

### How do you find relevant literature?

- Keyword searches — start broad, narrow with additional/combined keywords. _(Textbook illustration, Ch.4: searching PsycINFO for "television" → 6,671 hits; "television violence" → 156; "television children" → 17 — narrowing the keyword set narrows the hit count to something skimmable.)_
- Tutorials (learning the tools/field basics).
- **Citation chaining** — follow references backward from a paper you like, and forward via "cited by" to see who later built on it.
- Systematic reviews / meta-analyses — a single paper that has already aggregated a field for you.
- Discussing with peers.
- Discussions on online forums and social media — **use with caution**, quality/credibility varies.
- Research groups — your own lab, or others working in the area.
- _(Textbook additionally lists: professors — including outside your own university; textbooks, checking the subject index for your topic; and library databases such as PsycLit, PsycINFO, ProQuest, JSTOR Scholarly Journals, MedLine, and Sociological Abstracts.)_

### How do you read a research paper?

A paper has a standard anatomy — knowing it lets you read strategically instead of start-to-finish: **Title → Authors → Author affiliations → Abstract → Introduction → Materials and methods → Author summary → Results (with figures) → Discussion → Conclusion → Acknowledgements → Author contributions → References.** A common efficient reading order: Title → Abstract → Figures/Results → Discussion/Conclusion → (only then, if still relevant) Introduction and Methods in full.

### What is a research gap?

A research gap is **the missing piece in existing knowledge that your study aims to fill** — visualized in class as the one missing jigsaw piece surrounded by pieces representing "existing studies" and "known findings," with "limitations" pointing at the gap.

**Types of research gap** (two overlapping lists were shown in class; combined here as one reference set):

1. **Knowledge gap** — a broad lack of understanding/information on a topic, often because it's new, overlooked, or under-studied.
2. **Theoretical gap** — existing theories fail to adequately explain a phenomenon, or no suitable conceptual framework exists.
3. **Methodological gap** — existing studies relied on outdated, inappropriate, or narrow methods, suggesting a need for more robust or alternative approaches.
4. **Evidence gap** — a lack of strong, credible, high-quality findings to support claims, often from poor study design or insufficient data quality.
5. **Empirical gap** — theory exists, but little or no real-world observational/experimental data validates it.
6. **Population gap** — specific groups/demographics/communities are underrepresented in existing studies, leading to incomplete or biased findings.
7. **Geographical gap** — research is concentrated in certain regions/environments/contexts, leaving others unstudied.
8. **Practical gap** — research findings aren't applied in real-world practice — a disconnect between academic insight and implementation.
9. **Time gap** — documenting troubleshooting steps, solutions, and new knowledge as it's gained (a gap in what's actually been recorded over time).
10. **Evidence–practice gap** — strong evidence exists but isn't adopted in practice, due to barriers in knowledge transfer, policy, or industry adoption.

### Formulating the research problem

- Initially, the problem is stated in a broad, general way — you have to **"formulate a question(s)."**
- **Understand the problem thoroughly.**
- **Narrow it down** and phrase it in **operational terms** — turn the vague idea into something you can actually measure (the same "operationalization" step from the Wheel of Research above, and from the IV/DV discussion in §4).
- Once identified:
    - Be aware of the literature in the area.
    - Identify what is **different** between your work and others' — effectively, "find your gap" (see above).
    - Assess **feasibility**: feasibility = _f_(time, resources).
- _(Textbook, Ch.4, adds the reasons a research problem gets selected in the first place: to evaluate or advance a theory; to compare two or more competing theories; to address a real applied/practical problem; to resolve inconsistent or contradictory findings across existing studies — e.g. some studies find sleep-after-learning helps memory while others find it hurts, so new research compares the studies' procedures to find the source of the disagreement; or to extend/replicate prior findings — same effect in a new population or condition, or the same method repeated to check a surprising result holds up.)_

### After formulating the problem — write a brief summary that:

- States it **clearly and concisely**.
- Establishes **significance** — not trivial, not a repeat of previous work; if it _is_ a repeat, **justify why** (e.g. replication to check an implausible finding, or extension to a new population/condition — see textbook reasons above).
- **Delineates** (limits the scope) so it's practically investigable.
- Confirms it's **possible to obtain** the information/tools needed to explore the problem.
- Confirms it's **possible to draw conclusions** related to the problem — the point of research is to find some answers, i.e. you should be able to state potential hypotheses.
- Throughout, **stay an "active" reader/listener** — it makes it easier to generate alternative topics if the original plan doesn't pan out.

## 14. Exploratory vs. Hypothesis-Based Research

|                     | **Hypothesis-based (confirmatory)**                                            | **Exploratory**                                                                                        |
| ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Basis               | Based on scientific theory; finds out if the theory is supported by the facts  | Backed by no, or little-known, theory                                                                  |
| Motivating question | Can I reject H₀?                                                               | How can I optimally explain/describe variation in my data set?                                         |
| What it does        | Makes specific statements about causal relationships among a few key phenomena | Driven by hope and the chance of discovery                                                             |
| Method flavor       | —                                                                              | **Data mining**: post-hoc analyses, explanations, and hypotheses generated _after_ looking at the data |
| Reception           | Liked by funding/publishing agencies — clean, testable claims                  | Risk: a finding may not be a genuine new discovery, or may be a false positive found by chance         |

- The two are **not fully separate** — they form a cycle: **exploratory research** (pictured in class as Sherlock-Holmes-style clue-following) generates a **potential cause–effect** idea → this becomes a hypothesis fed into **confirmatory/hypothesis-based research** → whose result generates **new knowledge to explore**, looping back into further exploratory work. Exploratory findings often _become_ tomorrow's hypotheses, and confirmatory results often open up _new_ exploratory questions.
- **Can exploratory studies have a hypothesis?** The slides pose this as an open discussion question. The consistent answer, given the Grounded Theory approach already covered in §8 ("no hypothesis was tested up front — themes emerged from the analysis"), is that exploratory work does **not require** a hypothesis going in, though a loose research **problem** (§13) still frames what you're looking for.
- Related quote used in class (Einstein, paraphrased): work that already knows exactly what it's doing in advance would not really be called "research."

## 15. Hypotheses in Depth

### What is a hypothesis? (two textbook-style definitions shown in class)

1. **A proposition made as a basis for reasoning, without any assumption of its truth** — a "reasonable guess."
2. **A supposition or proposed explanation made on the basis of limited evidence, as a starting point for further investigation.**

_(Textbook, Ch.4, gives the practical version: a hypothesis is your educated guess about what should happen in a given situation under certain conditions. It should derive logically from prior findings or a theory — never just from what you personally think should happen. Good hypotheses have a clear rationale, an if–then structure, and a clear statement of the relationship between the variables involved, per §4's IV/DV material. Not every study needs a hypothesis — purely exploratory/descriptive work may not have one going in, §14.)_

A research problem funnels down into a hypothesis, which is typically split into two complementary statements:

- **H₀ (null hypothesis):** the hypothesis "deemed true until proven wrong" by the experimental data — the _no-effect / no-difference / no-relationship_ statement.
- **H₁ or Hₐ (alternative hypothesis):** the statement that there _is_ an effect/difference/relationship.

**Worked example (bio-fertilizer):**

- H₀: application of bio-fertilizer 'x' does **not** increase plant growth.
- H₁: application of bio-fertilizer 'x' **does** increase plant growth.

**Practice examples (writing an H₀ from a question):**

| Research question                                                        | Null hypothesis (H₀)                                        |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Are adults doing better at mathematics than teenagers?                   | Mathematical ability does not depend on age                 |
| Does the risk of a heart attack reduce with daily aspirin intake?        | A heart attack is not affected by the daily dose of aspirin |
| Are teenagers using cell phones to access the internet more than elders? | _(left as an in-class exercise)_                            |
| Are cats concerned about their food color?                               | _(left as an in-class exercise)_                            |

### Directional vs. non-directional hypotheses

Once there's reason to expect _some_ effect (i.e. to reject H₀), you can further specify **which direction** you expect:

- **Directional (one-tailed):** predicts the direction of the effect.
- **Non-directional (two-tailed):** predicts only that _some_ effect exists, without predicting its direction.

**Worked examples (same H₀ / directional-Hₐ / non-directional-Hₐ pattern each time):**

|Topic|H₀|Hₐ (directional)|Hₐ (non-directional)|
|---|---|---|---|
|Age & COVID mortality|Mortality rate is not dependent on age|Mortality rate of old people is greater than young people|Mortality rate affects age groups differently|
|Gender & COVID|COVID effects are not dependent on gender|COVID affects women less than men|COVID affects women and men differently|
|IDE & coding efficiency|Use of software X is not related to coding efficiency|Using software X improves coding efficiency|Using software X affects coding efficiency|

**Reading tip:** a directional hypothesis uses a comparative verb ("greater than," "less than," "improves"); a non-directional hypothesis uses a neutral verb ("affects," "differently," "is related to") — this exact wording difference is what a one-tailed vs. two-tailed statistical test later corresponds to.

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- §4's fish/IQ worked example (H1 vs H0 → experiment → "no significant difference between groups" → conclusion "only eating fish does not increase IQ") is a worked instance of _failing to reject_ H₀.
- §6/§9's Positivism, §7's Deduction, and quantitative research all converge on hypothesis-based (confirmatory) work; §8's Grounded Theory and §14's exploratory research converge on the no-hypothesis-up-front, data-first end of the spectrum.

## 16. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 4)

- **Descriptive research:** reports the current state of affairs; fact-finding; correlational, not causal; generates hypotheses.
- **Analytical research:** critically evaluates existing data/facts to explain _why_, not just _what_.
- **Fundamental (basic/pure) research:** knowledge for its own sake; broad generalizations/theory; same idea as "basic research" in §1's glossary entry.
- **Conceptual research:** developing/reinterpreting abstract ideas or theory through scholarly reasoning, without necessarily running an experiment.
- **Empirical research:** data-based, relies on experimentation; can reach conclusions without a pre-existing theory to test.
- **Wheel of Research:** cyclical model — theory/ideas → models → concepts → hypotheses → operationalization → data collection → descriptions/typologies → data analysis → explanation → generalization → assessing reliability → practical implications → back to theory. Quantitative research typically enters at "theory" (deductive); qualitative research typically enters at "empirical data" (inductive).
- **Research gap:** the missing piece in existing knowledge a study aims to fill; types include knowledge, theoretical, methodological, evidence, empirical, population, geographical, practical, time, and evidence–practice gaps.
- **Literature review:** the accumulated log of active/critical reading over time, periodically revisited; not a one-time step done before research starts, but an ongoing parallel activity.
- **Citation chaining:** following a paper's references backward, and its "cited by" list forward, to find related literature.
- **Exploratory research:** data-first, no (or little) prior theory; driven by chance of discovery; findings feed into future hypotheses; risk of false positives/data mining.
- **Hypothesis-based (confirmatory) research:** theory-first; tests whether facts support a specific, pre-stated hypothesis; motivating question is "can I reject H0?"
- **Null hypothesis (H0):** the no-effect/no-difference/no-relationship statement, treated as true until the data justify rejecting it.
- **Alternative hypothesis (H1/Ha):** the statement that an effect/difference/relationship exists; can be directional (predicts the direction, one-tailed) or non-directional (predicts only that an effect exists, two-tailed).
- **Feasibility (of a research problem):** a function of time and available resources — feasibility = f(time, resources).

## 17. Significance Testing & Hypothesis Testing

### The core question

Hypothesis testing exists to answer one practical question: **how sure are you that the results you found in an experiment are actually true**, rather than a fluke of the particular sample you happened to collect?

### What is significance testing?

- **Significance testing is about finding the probability that a result would have occurred by chance if there is, in fact, no effect** — i.e., if the null hypothesis (H₀, §15) is actually true.
- Picture a bell curve of all possible results **under the assumption that H₀ is true**. The peak is the **true value under the null hypothesis and most likely observation**. Results far out in either tail are **very unlikely observations** if H₀ is really true.
- A **95% statistical significance threshold** marks off the outer edge of that curve (conventionally the most common threshold, though not the only one used). If your **observed result** falls beyond that threshold — out in the unlikely tail — the area of the curve beyond it is your **observed p-value (statistical significance)**.
- Intuition: if your result would be very unlikely to occur by chance when there's truly no effect, but you _did_ observe it, that's evidence the "no effect" assumption (H₀) is probably wrong.

### The p-value (for self: think of it in %)

- **A p-value is a measure of the probability that an observed difference could have occurred just by random chance, assuming the null hypothesis is true.**
- **The lower the p-value, the greater the statistical significance of the observed difference.**
- **A p-value is a measure of how much evidence you have _against_ H₀** — it is not a measure of how _big_ or how _important_ the effect is, and it is not the probability that H₀ is true.
- **Rough interpretation scale** (shown in class as a tongue-in-cheek chart, useful as a memory aid for how p-values are conventionally read):

| p-value    | Conventional interpretation                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ---------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| ≤ 0.001    | Highly significant                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| 0.01–0.03  | Highly significant                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| 0.04–0.049 | Significant                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| 0.050      | "Redo calculations" (right at the boundary)                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| 0.051–0.06 | "On the edge of significance"                                                                                                                                                                                                 |
| 0.07–0.09  | "Highly suggestive, significant at the p<0.10 level"                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ≥ 0.1      | Not significant ("interesting subgroup analysis" — i.e., often a sign of going looking for _some_ significant result after the fact, a data-mining/false-positive risk connecting back to §14's Exploratory Research caution) |

- **Practical exam skill — "reject or not reject H₀?":** worked example: _"There is a relationship between age and job satisfaction."_ Framed as: H₀ = "there is **no** relationship between age and job satisfaction." If your significance test gives you a low enough p-value (below your threshold, e.g., 0.05), the decision is **reject H₀** ("Yes!" — there does appear to be a relationship). If the p-value is above threshold, the decision is **fail to reject H₀** — you don't have enough evidence to say the relationship exists, though this does **not** prove H₀ is true (same "support, not proof" logic as §4).
- **Population vs. sample:** significance testing exists precisely because you almost never have access to the whole **population** — you only have a **sample** drawn from it. The p-value quantifies how confident you can be that a pattern seen in your _sample_ reflects something real in the _population_, rather than being an artifact of which particular people happened to end up in your sample.

### The two ways a decision can go wrong: Type I and Type II errors**

Hypothesis testing is framed as a 2×2 decision table crossing **your decision based on the sample** against **the (unknown, in practice) truth about the population**:

|                       | **H₀ true (in reality)**                                                                                                          | **Hₐ true (in reality)**                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| --------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reject H₀**         | **Type I error (α)** — "false positive": you conclude there's an effect when there really isn't one                               | **Correct decision — Specificity (1−α)**: you correctly detect a real effect _(note: the slide labels this cell "Specificity," though this cell is more standardly called statistical power/a true positive; see the note below)_ |
| **Fail to reject H₀** | **Correct decision — Sensitivity/Power (1−β)** _(as labelled in the slide; standard terminology would call this a true negative)_ | **Type II error (β)** — "false negative": you fail to detect an effect that's really there                                                                                                                                        |

**A note on the slide's labelling vs. standard statistical terminology:** the class slide's placement of "Specificity" and "Sensitivity/Power" swaps the usual convention (normally Power/Sensitivity = correctly rejecting H₀ when Hₐ is true, and Specificity = correctly failing to reject H₀ when H₀ is true). Learn the table exactly as your instructor presented it for the exam, but be aware that in most textbooks and in the diagnostic-test framing below, **Power (Sensitivity)** refers to correctly detecting a true effect/true positive, and **Specificity** refers to correctly identifying a true negative. If a question asks you to define these terms rather than just fill in the class's table, use the standard definitions:

- **α (alpha):** the Type I error rate — the probability of rejecting H₀ when it's actually true. This is exactly the significance threshold (e.g., 0.05) you set before running the test.
- **β (beta):** the Type II error rate — the probability of failing to reject H₀ when Hₐ is actually true.
- **Power = 1 − β:** the probability of correctly detecting a real effect when one exists.

**Memory aid:** Type I error is like a smoke alarm going off when there's no fire (false alarm); Type II error is like a smoke alarm staying silent while the house is actually burning (missed detection). Which error is "worse" depends on context — e.g., in a medical test, a Type II error (missing a real disease) is often far more costly than a Type I error (a false alarm that gets ruled out on follow-up testing).

### The confusion-matrix version (diagnostic-test framing)

The same 2×2 logic is re-drawn using a medical diagnostic-test analogy — mapping "test result" against "actual disease status":

| |**Disease present**|**Disease absent**|
|---|---|---|
|**Test positive**|**TP** (True Positive)|**FP** (False Positive)|
|**Test negative**|**FN** (False Negative)|**TN** (True Negative)|

This maps directly onto the hypothesis-testing table: "test positive" ≈ "reject H₀," "disease present" ≈ "Hₐ true." So **FP ≈ Type I error**, **FN ≈ Type II error**.

**Derived metrics** (all built from TP/FP/FN/TN) — useful vocabulary for reading papers or being asked to define a term on the exam:

| Metric                           | Formula                                 | What it captures                                                                                     |
| -------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **False Positive Rate (FPR)**    | FP / (TN + FP)                          | Of everyone who's actually H₀-true/disease-free, what fraction got a false alarm                     |
| **Specificity (TNR)**            | 1 − FPR                                 | Of everyone who's actually H₀-true/disease-free, what fraction were correctly cleared                |
| **Power / Recall / Sensitivity** | TP / (TP + FN) = 1 − Type II error rate | Of everyone who actually has the effect/disease, what fraction did you correctly catch               |
| **False Omission Rate (FOR)**    | FN / (TN + FN)                          | Of everyone you _cleared_ (failed to reject H₀ / tested negative), what fraction were actually wrong |
| **False Discovery Rate (FDR)**   | FP / (FP + TP)                          | Of everyone you _flagged_ (rejected H₀ / tested positive), what fraction were actually wrong         |
| **Precision**                    | 1 − FDR                                 | Of everyone you flagged, what fraction were correctly flagged                                        |

**Key distinction to keep straight for the exam:** **α/Type I/FPR-side metrics** are about your **false-alarm rate among true negatives**; **β/Type II/Power-side metrics** are about your **miss rate (or catch rate) among true positives**. A significance threshold (e.g., p < 0.05) directly controls α — it does **not** directly control β/power; power depends additionally on sample size and effect size, which is why "the study is well-powered" and "the result is statistically significant" are two different claims.

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- This section is the formal statistical machinery underneath §15's H₀/H₁ material — §15 taught you how to _write_ a null and alternative hypothesis; this section teaches you how a study actually _decides_ whether to reject that H₀ once data comes in.
- The **"support, not proof"** principle from §4 is exactly why failing to reject H₀ is phrased as "fail to reject," never "H₀ is proven true" — a non-significant result is just insufficient evidence against H₀, not positive proof that there's truly no effect (this is precisely a potential **Type II error** situation: maybe there _is_ an effect, but your study didn't have the power to detect it).
- The **p ≥ 0.1 "interesting subgroup analysis"** joke in the interpretation table is a direct callback to the **confirmation bias / data-mining** risk discussed in §4 and §14 — going back into your data to find _some_ subgroup where the result looks significant, after the main test came back non-significant, is a classic way to manufacture false positives.

## 18. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 5)

- **Significance testing:** the process of finding the probability that an observed result would occur by chance if the null hypothesis (H₀) is true.
- **p-value:** the probability that an observed difference could have occurred by random chance, assuming H₀ is true; lower p-values indicate stronger evidence against H₀. Not a measure of effect size or the probability H₀ is true.
- **Significance threshold (α):** the p-value cutoff (conventionally 0.05, i.e., a 95% significance threshold) below which a result is considered statistically significant enough to reject H₀.
- **Type I error (α, false positive):** rejecting H₀ when it is actually true — concluding there's an effect when there isn't one.
- **Type II error (β, false negative):** failing to reject H₀ when Hₐ is actually true — missing a real effect.
- **Power (1 − β):** the probability of correctly detecting a real effect (correctly rejecting H₀ when Hₐ is true); depends on sample size, effect size, and the chosen significance threshold, not on the threshold alone.
- **Specificity (1 − FPR / True Negative Rate):** the proportion of true negatives (H₀-true cases) correctly identified as such.
- **Sensitivity / Recall:** the proportion of true positives (Hₐ-true cases) correctly identified as such; equivalent to power.
- **False Positive Rate (FPR):** proportion of true-negative cases incorrectly flagged as positive.
- **False Discovery Rate (FDR):** proportion of all positive (rejected-H₀) results that are actually false positives; Precision = 1 − FDR.
- **False Omission Rate (FOR):** proportion of all negative (failed-to-reject-H₀) results that are actually false negatives.
- **Confusion matrix (TP/FP/FN/TN):** a 2×2 table cross-tabulating a test's decision against ground truth, from which all the above rates are derived; maps directly onto the H₀/Hₐ hypothesis-testing decision table.

## 19. Designing an Experiment

### Starting point for designing an experiment

Every experiment design starts from the same three moves, sitting on top of a stated **hypothesis** and clear **objectives** (pictured in class as a "puzzle-piece path to objectives"):

1. **Identify Variables** — independent, dependent, controlled (see below).
2. **Identify Conditions** — the specific levels/groups being compared (e.g., three groups being weighed, illustrating that repeated/averaged measurement reduces random error).
3. **Identify Setting** — lab/controlled vs. naturalistic/field (binoculars vs. lab-door icons used in class to represent this choice).

### Independent and dependent variables — recap and drill

This restates and drills §5's IV/DV material with new worked examples:

- **Independent variable (IV):** what you change/manipulate.
- **Dependent variable (DV):** what you observe/measure.
- **Controlled variable:** what you keep the same across conditions.

**Class drill examples (identify IV/DV for each):**

- _"The influence of the color of the cup on consumers' perception of a hot beverage"_ (Piqueras-Fiszman & Spence) → IV = cup color; DV = perceived taste/temperature of the beverage.
- _"Severity of gambling is associated with severity of depressive symptoms in pathological gamblers"_ (Thomsen et al.) → note the _"associated with"_ phrasing — this is a **correlational** study title, not an experimental one, so there's no manipulated IV in the strict sense; gambling severity and depressive symptom severity are both measured variables.
- _Teaching efficacy ↔ students' interest level_ (image: one student asleep, one engaged) → IV = teaching efficacy/method; DV = student interest level; **Extraneous variable?** — things like how much sleep each student got, their existing interest in the subject, or time of day could all masquerade as an effect of teaching efficacy.

### Extraneous variables

**Extraneous variables** are undesirable variables that influence the relationship between the variables an experimenter is examining — they affect the outcome of an experiment even though they aren't the variables actually of interest. Three types:

| Type                      | Definition                                                                                                                                                     | Example                                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Random**                | Creates _unpredictable_ error in measurement; unsystematic                                                                                                     | A thermostat going on/off unpredictably; experimental apparatus behaving slightly differently trial to trial; a watering can watering a plant unevenly                                              |
| **Constant (systematic)** | A source of constant/systematic error that causes measurements to deviate _consistently_ from their true value                                                 | A clinical trial where all participants happen to be tested only during the day, or an AC unit that's only running in one of two buildings being compared (Mon vs. Tue conditions)                  |
| **Confounding**           | An extraneous variable that changes **systematically along with the IV** you're studying — it therefore offers an **alternative explanation** for your results | Campaign ad content (IV) → Vote (DV), confounded by political preference, gender, knowledge of issues, religion, home community, ethnicity, age — any of which could independently predict the vote |

**Why confounds matter — three specific failure modes:**

- You may **assume the IV has an effect when it doesn't** (the confound was actually driving the result).
- You may **assume an effect doesn't occur when it does** (the confound masked a real IV effect).
- You may **wrongly identify the causal component of the IV** (you attribute the effect to the wrong part of what you manipulated).

**How to avoid confounds (the practical checklist):**

- Manipulate **only** the IV; control all other variables, so that any change in the DV must be due to the IV.
- Provide an **identical setting/treatment** for all subjects: standardized instructions and procedures, same environment, etc.
- **Don't give hints** about the "right" way of responding (this connects to Demand Characteristics, below).

_(Textbook, Ch.1, gives a canonical illustration of exactly this failure mode: two day-care centers are compared, one shown a violent TV program and one a nonviolent program, with aggressive behavior afterward as the DV. Even if the two centers differ significantly on aggression, you cannot conclude the TV content caused it — the centers likely differ in staff quality, activities, typical programming, and parents' income/education, any of which is a confound offering an alternative explanation. This is the same logic as the campaign-ad and Ayurveda/COVID examples in §4.)_

### Comparison studies

A **comparison study** looks at different groups/tools/methods/categories of people and how they differ with respect to certain variables — it examines the effect of the IV on the DV **via grouping**:

- **Experimental group:** receives manipulation of the IV.
- **Baseline/control group:** no manipulation of the IV.

**Typical comparative-study structure:** Measurement of DV (baseline) → Treatment (IV) _[experimental group]_ / No treatment _[control group]_ → Measurement of DV (post) → **compare the two groups' change** to find out if the IV caused an effect in the DV.

_Worked example: teaching programming with vs. without an automated tool that gives feedback_ — experimental group gets the tool (the IV/"treatment"), control group doesn't; both are measured on the DV before and after.

### Main types of comparison studies (time-horizon axis — connects to §2's Research Onion)

- **Cross-sectional:** multiple different people/groups measured at (roughly) one point in time.
- **Longitudinal:** the same people/group measured repeatedly over time.
- **Combination of both:** overlapping cross-sectional snapshots taken at multiple time points (different groups measured at each of several time points).

**Worked example — Post-Stroke: Does music listening expedite recovery?**

- 60 stroke patients tested at baseline (neuropsychological testing, MEG & MRI, ~1 week post-stroke), then **randomly allocated to three groups (n=20 each)**: music group (standard care + self-selected music, min. 1 h/day), audio-book group (standard care + self-selected audiobooks, min. 1 h/day), and control group (standard care only). Follow-up testing at 3 months and 6 months (with some drop-outs: n=55 at 3 months, n=54 at 6 months).
- **Class discussion question: is this cross-sectional, longitudinal, or a combination?** — since the _same_ 60 (later 54) patients are followed and re-tested at three separate time points (baseline, 3 months, 6 months), this is a **longitudinal** design (with three comparison groups running in parallel).**

### Types of Experiments

Four broad categories, from most to least controlled — matching the "controlled → quasi → non-experimental / field" spectrum first introduced under §5's Project Types:

**1. Controlled ("true") experiments**

- Complete control over the manipulation of the IV and control of all other relevant variables.
- Have **randomly assigned** participants to a condition (if using independent samples).
- Aim to **establish cause and effect**.
- _Example: choosing the ideal location to place an advertisement on a webpage — testing ad placement (IV) on click-through/attention (DV) with full control over layout._
- **Cons:** conducted in environments that are not natural for participants (non-contextual), so the behavior observed might not reflect what happens in real life.

**2. Quasi-experiments** ("almost" controlled)

- Do **not** have complete control over all central variables.
- **Random allocation of participants is not possible.**
- The IV in question is often something that is an **innate characteristic** (e.g., personality, gender) rather than something you can randomly assign.
- _Single-group design example:_ Students → participate in an exchange program (IV) → measure attitudes toward immigrants (DV) — there's no random assignment to "exchange program" vs. "no exchange program," so any pre-existing differences between students who chose to go and those who didn't are a threat to the conclusion.
- _True vs. quasi field experiment, side by side:_ **True field experiment** — randomly assigning different neighborhoods to receive different styles of music in public spaces, then measuring changes in social interaction (random assignment preserved, just done in the field). **Quasi field experiment** — choosing neighborhoods that _already_ differ in music exposure and comparing them (no randomization) — the neighborhoods might also differ in income, demographics, etc., which are confounds.

**3. Non-experiments ("natural" experiments)**

- For **descriptive or analytical research** (§12).
- Relies on **observation** to interpret and come to a conclusion.
- **Lacks manipulation of the IV**, typically due to **ethical considerations** (e.g., you can't ethically assign people to sleep-deprivation or violent-video-game conditions long-term).
- _Examples: epidemiological settings; comparing people who already eat organically vs. those who don't on a health assessment; observing what happens to people who already don't sleep enough and their rates of depression; a PUBG-style "does playing violent games change behavior" question that can only be studied by comparing existing players vs. non-players, not by randomly forcing people to play._

**4. Field Research (a non-experimental category with its own five sub-methods)**

- Conducted in the **"real" world / outside the laboratory**, in the **natural environment** of the people studied.
- Observes and **captures naturally occurring behavior**.
- It **may** be possible to manipulate the IV and use random allocation — on rare occasions field research can be considered a **"true" experiment** — but it's generally **not easy to minimize the effects of other variables** and control for them.
- Five sub-methods, arranged in a cycle: **Ethnography, Qualitative Interviews, Direct Observation, Participant Observation, Case Study.**
- **Observational and Descriptive** in character.

**Field Research — the four observer/participant roles:**

| Role                                      | Description                                         |
| ----------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| **Complete observer (detached)**          | No participation, role not revealed                 |
| **Observer as (known) participant**       | No participation, but role is revealed to the group |
| **Complete participant (undercover/spy)** | Full participation, role not revealed               |
| **Participant as (known) observer**       | Full participation, and role is revealed            |

**When is participant observation especially useful?**

- **Studying subcultures or groups with unique practices or beliefs:** participant observation fosters a deep, intimate understanding of the beliefs, values, and practices of a group from an **insider's perspective**; especially useful for **marginalized groups** or groups **resistant to observation**.
- **Studying complex social interactions:** immersing yourself in the group lets you observe firsthand and gain a more nuanced understanding of how interactions actually flow (rather than trying to infer it from outside).
- **Studying behaviors/practices difficult to self-report:** participants may be unwilling or unable to accurately report their own behavior; direct observation gets around this, improving data accuracy.
- _Worked examples discussed in class:_ "Scaling Classroom IT Skill Tutoring: A Case Study from India" (Joshi, Joshi, & Rangaswamy, CHI 2018) — a case study of how Ameerpet's informal IT-tuition ecosystem works; and a discussion prompt about gig-economy delivery workers (Swiggy) and fingerprint/biometric authentication — both framed as "what type of experiment/field method would this be?" exercises.

### Laboratory studies vs. field studies — trade-offs

|                   | **Laboratory studies**                                                                       | **Field studies**                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Advantages**    | Greater control over variables; **high internal validity**                                   | **High ecological/external validity**; few demand characteristics                                                                               |
| **Disadvantages** | Narrow definition of IV and DV; **low generalizability**; artificial; demand characteristics | Lack of control, no random allocation; **more confounds**; can be expensive and time-consuming; ethical issues (e.g., lack of informed consent) |

- **Internal validity** = confidence that the manipulated IV, not a confound, caused the observed effect in the DV (highest in tightly controlled lab experiments — see §16's glossary entry).
- **External/ecological validity** = how well findings generalize beyond the specific study setting to real life (highest in field studies).
- This is the same fundamental trade-off already illustrated by the six "heat makes people aggressive" designs in §4 — tightly controlled lab manipulations buy you causal certainty at the cost of realism, while field/naturalistic methods buy you realism at the cost of causal certainty.

### Demand characteristics

**Demand characteristics** = _"pleasing the experimenter"_ — cues in the experimental setup (the way the study is presented, the setting, the experimenter's behavior) that inadvertently signal to participants what response is "expected," causing them to (consciously or not) alter their behavior to match. This is why the "don't give hints about the right way of responding" rule under Confounding Variables above matters, and why lab settings — despite their internal-validity advantages — carry this as a specific extra risk (illustrated in class with an image referencing the Stanford Prison Experiment's guard/prisoner role-conformity).

### Comparison table: five research-design methodologies (from a summary slide)**

| Feature                           | True Experiment                                                                 | Field Experiment                                                                   | Quasi-Experiment                                                              | Natural Experiment                                                           | Standard Non-Experiment                                                |
| --------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Researcher manipulates IV?        | Yes (strict control)                                                            | Yes (in real world)                                                                | Yes (hands-on intervention)                                                   | No (occurs naturally)                                                        | No (observational only)                                                |
| Environment                       | Artificial lab setting                                                          | Real-world setting                                                                 | Real-world or clinic                                                          | Real-world setting                                                           | Real-world setting                                                     |
| How groups are formed             | Strict random assignment                                                        | Strict random assignment                                                           | Pre-existing intact groups (e.g., schools, age brackets)                      | Forced by an outside, unpredictable event                                    | Self-selected / pre-existing traits                                    |
| Control over extraneous variables | High                                                                            | Moderate                                                                           | Moderate to Low                                                               | Low                                                                          | Very Low                                                               |
| Ability to prove causation        | Highest                                                                         | High                                                                               | Moderate                                                                      | Moderate ("as-if" random)                                                    | Lowest (mostly correlational)                                          |
| Example                           | Testing a drug by assigning participants to a pill or placebo group in a clinic | Testing a new teaching method by randomly assigning different classrooms to use it | Giving a new therapy program to Clinic A, using Clinic B as the control group | Studying health outcomes after a sudden city-wide cigarette ban takes effect | Surveying lifelong smokers about their daily habits and overall health |

**Key exam skill:** given any scenario, you should be able to (1) name which of these five categories it belongs to, (2) identify the IV and DV, (3) identify the setting, and (4) flag likely confounds — this is exactly the drill format used in the four "guess the experiment type" slides in class (Bus vs. Taxi Drivers/MRI; Serotonin & the brain; Saint Helena TV study; Light Therapy for depression). For instance:

- _Bus vs. Taxi Drivers (MRI comparison of brain structure by driving experience)_ → **Quasi-experiment** (driving experience/occupation is an existing trait, not randomly assigned) in a **lab/clinical setting** (MRI scanner).
- _Saint Helena Study (TV introduced to an island, behavior measured before/after)_ → **Natural experiment** (the TV introduction was a real-world event the researchers didn't control, though they did plan to measure around it) in a **field setting**.
- _Light Therapy for depression (patients randomly assigned to red/bright/soft light conditions in their own bedrooms)_ → **True or field experiment** depending on how strictly "random assignment in a real-world setting" is read — it has random assignment (true-experiment-like) but takes place in participants' own homes (field-like), making it a good discussion case for the blurry line between "true" and "field" experiments.

## 20. Operationalization

### What is operationalization?

- **op·er·a·tion·a·lize** (verb): removing ambiguity in written work and research by defining all relevant variables such that they can be **objectively measured**.
- More formally: **a process of defining the measurement of a phenomenon that is not directly measurable**, though its existence is implied by other phenomena — i.e., the **processes or steps taken to measure an abstract concept in terms of its observable, measurable characteristics**.
- This is the same concept already introduced via the Wheel of Research (§12) and the Formulating-the-Research-Problem section (§13) — Lecture 6 is essentially the deep dive on that one step.

### Conceptualization vs. Operationalization

|              | **Conceptualization**                                     | **Operationalization**                                                                                                              |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What it is   | The process of **defining or specifying concepts**        | The process by which a researcher precisely specifies **how a concept will be measured**                                            |
| Involves     | Defining/specifying what we mean when using certain terms | Developing specific research definitions that will bring about empirical observations representing those concepts in the real world |
| Main purpose | Refining and specifying **abstract concepts**             | Removing vagueness and making sure concepts are **measurable**                                                                      |
| Order        | **First** step in the measurement process                 | **Second** step in the measurement process                                                                                          |

**Practice table (operationalize the variable):**

| The variable                             | The operationalized variable                                                            |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Aggression from children in a playground | _(exercise — e.g., number of hitting/pushing incidents observed per 10-minute period)_  |
| Problem solving ability                  | _(exercise — e.g., number of puzzles correctly solved within a time limit)_             |

### Worked example — "People perform worse when watched" (Julie & Pete's driving conversation)

A full case study walking through the entire pipeline from an everyday claim to a testable design — ties together §5 (IV/DV), §15 (hypotheses), and §19 (experiment design) into one worked example:

**The everyday observation (a conversation transcript used as prompt):** Julie complains she drives smoothly alone but makes mistakes (crashing gears, stalling) when her friend Susie is in the car, and generalizes: _"I'm sure people perform worse when someone important's watching them."_

**Step 1 — State the hypothesis:** Hₐ = _People's performance is affected when they are being watched while driving._

**Step 2 — Identify DV and IV within the hypothesis sentence:**

- **DV** = **performance** (the thing being measured/observed).
- **IV** = **being watched** (the thing being manipulated/compared).

**Step 3 — Identify Setting, Task/Condition, Sample:**

- **Setting:** lab/controlled.
- **Task/Condition:** initially left open ("?"), then specified as a **sensorimotor task** (a wire-loop "steady-hand" style apparatus where touching the wire triggers an error) — chosen because it's a task with an objective, easily-measurable performance criterion, standing in for "driving" without needing an actual car.
- **Sample:** to be determined (raises the general question of population/sampling, a Research-Onion "Techniques & Procedures" concern from §2).
- **Comparison study or single trial?** — posed as an open design decision, connecting back to §19's Comparison Studies section: do you compare one group across two conditions (repeated measures) or two different groups (independent measures)? This is answered next.

**Step 4 — Operationalize DV and IV concretely:**

- **DV → "a measure of performance"** operationalized as **number of errors** (touching the wire) — pictured as a "NO ERRORS" icon, i.e., lower error count = better performance.
- **IV → "being watched"** operationalized as two conditions: **condition A** = an audience present (people watching, wearing 3D-glasses in the class's illustration) vs. **condition B** = an empty room/no audience.

**Step 5 — Choose Sample / design type:** This is where the **within-subjects vs. between-subjects** decision (below) gets made explicit, using the same task/audience/performance-measure setup.

### Within-subjects vs. Between-subjects design

|                         | **Within subjects** (repeated measures / related design)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | **Between subjects** (independent measures / unrelated design)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ----------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Setup                   | The **same participants** do the sensorimotor task under **both** condition A (audience) and condition B (no audience)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                             | **Different participants** are assigned to condition A **or** condition B, not both                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| Key problem             | **Order effects** (a constant/systematic error — e.g., practice/learning or fatigue changes performance across trials regardless of condition)                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Need to **match participants** across groups, and need **roughly twice as many** participants overall                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     |
| Solution to the problem | **Counterbalancing** — alternate the order across participants (AB, BA, BA, BA, AB, …), or **complex counterbalancing (ABBA)** — each participant does A, B, B, A in that order, and their score for condition A is taken as the mean of their two A trials (same for B); also controlling for **elapsed time** between trials to counter learning/fatigue effects | **Matched-pair design** — pair participants on relevant characteristics before assigning them to different conditions. Problem: matched pairs are **not easy to find, time-consuming**, and variance may still differ between the two groups. Solution: use appropriate **statistical tests (e.g., permutation tests)** that don't assume the groups are identical in every other respect                 |
| Pros                    | **Fewer participants** needed overall; **minimizes random error** (each person acts as their own baseline)                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                         | **Minimizes learning/transfer effects across conditions** (no risk that doing condition A changes how someone does in condition B); **less time needed per participant**                                                                                                                                                                                                                                  |
| Cons                    | Not suitable for some experiments; the **aim of the research becomes obvious** to participants after seeing both conditions (raising demand-characteristics risk, §19); **availability** of participants for all conditions can be limited                                                                                                                         | Needing **twice as many participants**; harder to isolate the pure effect of the IV from pre-existing individual differences between the two separate groups                                                                                                                                                                                                                                              |
| When especially handy   | Default choice when order effects can be adequately controlled                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | **Post-hoc evaluation/validation** — e.g., first study a group of musicians in an fMRI scanner (expensive, low-n, hard to repeat), then **separately** validate/extend the finding with a larger, cheaper between-subjects study (e.g., headphone-listening group vs. a comparison group) — using the two designs as complementary stages of the same research program rather than a single design choice |

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- The IV/DV/Controlled-variable framing here is the practical, worked-example version of the abstract definitions already given in §5.
- The Julie/Pete example is a complete, compressed walk-through of the **Wheel of Research** (§12): everyday observation → hypothesis → operationalization → data collection design — all in one scenario.
- Within-subjects vs. between-subjects is the concrete version of the "Comparison Studies" experimental-group-vs-control-group structure from §19, applied to the question of _how the same two conditions get assigned to people_ rather than just _that_ there are two conditions.
- Order effects and counterbalancing are a specific case of the **random/constant extraneous variables** distinction from §19 — an order effect is a _constant_ (systematic) source of error unless counterbalancing is used to spread it out and neutralize it.

## 21. Additional Glossary Terms (Lectures 6 & 7)

- **Independent variable (IV) / Dependent variable (DV) / Controlled variable:** what you change / what you observe / what you keep the same — the three components identified at the start of any experiment design.
- **Extraneous variable:** an undesirable variable, other than the IV, that influences the outcome of an experiment; subtypes are random, constant, and confounding.
- **Random variable (as extraneous-variable type):** creates unpredictable, unsystematic error in measurement.
- **Constant variable (as extraneous-variable type):** a source of constant/systematic error causing measurements to deviate consistently from their true value.
- **Confounding variable:** an extraneous variable that changes systematically along with the IV, providing an alternative explanation for the results; can cause false positives, false negatives, or misattributed causal components.
- **Comparison study:** examines the effect of an IV on a DV by comparing an experimental group (receives the IV manipulation) against a baseline/control group (does not).
- **Cross-sectional / longitudinal / combination design:** comparison studies structured as one-time snapshots across groups, repeated measurement of the same group over time, or a mix of both.
- **True (controlled) experiment:** complete control over IV manipulation and other variables, random assignment where applicable, aims to establish cause and effect.
- **Quasi-experiment:** lacks complete control over central variables and random allocation; the IV is often an innate/pre-existing characteristic (e.g., personality, gender, occupation).
- **Non-experiment ("natural" experiment):** descriptive/analytical research relying on observation, with no manipulation of the IV, typically for ethical reasons.
- **Field research:** research conducted in participants' natural environment rather than a lab; includes ethnography, qualitative interviews, direct observation, participant observation, and case study.
- **Participant observation roles:** complete observer (detached, role hidden), observer as known participant (role revealed, no participation), complete participant (undercover), participant as known observer (role revealed, participates).
- **Internal validity:** confidence that the IV, not a confound, caused the effect in the DV; typically highest in controlled lab experiments.
- **External/ecological validity:** how well findings generalize beyond the specific study sample/setting; typically highest in field studies.
- **Demand characteristics:** cues in an experimental setting that lead participants to guess the study's purpose and adjust their behavior to "please the experimenter."
- **Operationalization:** the process of defining how an abstract, not-directly-measurable phenomenon will be measured in terms of observable, measurable characteristics; the second step in the measurement process, after conceptualization.
- **Conceptualization:** the first step in the measurement process — defining or specifying what a concept/term means, before deciding how to measure it.
- **Within-subjects (repeated measures / related) design:** the same participants experience all conditions; main risk is order effects, addressed via counterbalancing.
- **Between-subjects (independent measures / unrelated) design:** different participants are assigned to different conditions; main cost is needing more participants and matching them, addressed via matched-pair designs or appropriate statistical tests.
- **Counterbalancing / complex counterbalancing (ABBA):** techniques for spreading or neutralizing order effects across conditions in a within-subjects design.
- **Matched-pair design:** pairing participants on relevant traits before assigning them to different between-subjects conditions, to reduce the confound of pre-existing individual differences.

## 22. Factorial Design

### Why go beyond one IV?

Every design covered through §19–21 involved **one IV and one DV** (or, at most, one IV compared across two conditions). But almost no real behavior has a single cause. A **factorial design** lets a researcher study **two or more independent variables at the same time**, in the *same* study, which does something a series of separate one-IV studies cannot do: it reveals whether the IVs **interact**.

- **Factor** = an independent variable (when there is more than one IV, each one is called a "factor").
- **Level** = one of the specific conditions/values that a factor can take.
- Example used throughout the slides: does an **audience** (condition A = people watching vs. condition B = empty room) affect performance on a task, and does this depend on whether the person is an **introvert or extrovert**? Here, "audience type" and "personality type" are the two factors; each has 2 levels, so there are **2 × 2 = 4 conditions** (cells): {audience, introvert}, {audience, extrovert}, {no audience, introvert}, {no audience, extrovert}.

### Notation and total number of conditions

- A factorial design is named by listing the number of levels of each factor, separated by "×": **2 × 2**, **2 × 3**, **3 × 4**, **2 × 2 × 2**, etc.
- **Total number of conditions (cells) = the product of the number of levels of every factor.**
  - 2 × 2 → 4 conditions
  - 2 × 3 → 6 conditions
  - 3 × 4 → 12 conditions
  - 2 × 2 × 2 → 8 conditions
- **Practice (from slides):**
  - *Scenario A:* type of music (silence / instrumental / lyrical) × task difficulty (easy / difficult) → this is a **3 × 2 design**, **6 total conditions**.
  - *Scenario B:* feedback (positive / negative) × expertise (novice / intermediate / expert) → this is a **2 × 3 design**, **6 total conditions**.
  - Note that the order you list the factors in doesn't change the total (3×2 and 2×3 both give 6), but by convention the factor mentioned first in the description is usually listed first.
- The idea generalizes to any number of factors: a **Type of Model × Type of Dataset × Type of Features** design (an ML-flavored example from the slides) is a three-way factorial design, visualized as a "stack" of 2×2 grids (one grid per level of the third factor).

### Main Effects

- A **main effect** = the effect of **one factor on the DV, averaged/collapsed across all levels of the other factor(s)**.
- Visually (line graphs of means): if you **ignore which line is which** and just ask "does the value on the y-axis change as you move along the x-axis?", you're asking about the main effect of the x-axis variable. If you ask "is one line consistently higher than the other, regardless of x-axis position?", you're asking about the main effect of the variable represented by line color/style (e.g., introvert vs extrovert).
- **A main effect is "consistent"** — the slides define it as: "a particular IV has a consistent effect" across the levels of the other IV. E.g., if performance (no. of errors) is lower for condition B (empty room) than condition A (audience) for **both** introverts and extraverts, and by roughly the same amount, that's a clean main effect of "audience" with no interaction.

 vertical avg at ends of line1 and line2, check var across x axis. if present -> iv1 dep
 horizontal avg of line itself, if one higher abt other -> iv2 dep 
### Interactions

- An **interaction** = **the effect of one IV depends on (changes across) the level of the other IV.**
- This is the entire *added value* of a factorial design over running two separate one-IV studies — a pair of one-IV studies could each detect a main effect, but **neither could ever reveal an interaction**, because an interaction is a claim about how the two IVs combine.
- Interactions show up graphically as **non-parallel lines**. If two lines are parallel (even if not perfectly overlapping and even if they both slope up or down), there's no interaction — just possibly two main effects. If the lines **converge, cross, diverge, or one is flat while the other slopes**, that is evidence of an interaction.
  - Slide example: extrovert and introvert lines both decline from condition A to B, roughly in parallel → **no interaction, but a possible main effect of condition** (and a possible main effect of personality if one line sits above the other throughout).
  - Slide example ("interaction" version): the extrovert line stays roughly flat/slightly declines while the introvert line rises sharply from A to B, so the two lines cross → **an interaction** — the effect of the audience condition is different (even opposite in direction) depending on personality type.
- **From the textbook (Ch. 13):** a classic example is combining psychotherapy and drug treatment for mental illness. Neither psychotherapy alone nor drug treatment alone may improve patients much (weak/no main effects), but the **combination** of both may produce a large improvement — an interaction that a single-IV study of "drug vs. no drug" or "therapy vs. no therapy" could never reveal on its own.
- **Recap table from slides — worked interpretations:**

| Study | Main Effect of Factor 1 | Main Effect of Factor 2 | Interaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Driving performance (day/night × cell phone/no cell phone) | Yes: better during the day | Yes: better without a cell phone | No/weak — the cell-phone penalty looks similar day and night |
| Psychotherapy (Type A vs Type B × length of treatment) | Yes: symptoms generally decrease with length | Little/no clear overall effect | Yes: strong interaction — Type A improves faster over time and eventually beats Type B, which plateaus/reverses slightly |

- **Simple effect** (textbook term, not heavily used in the slides but useful for exam answers): when a significant "main effect" is actually only true at *one* level of the other factor (i.e., it's driven entirely by the interaction), the effect at that one level is called a **simple effect**, and reporting it as if it were a clean, general main effect would be misleading. Whenever an interaction is significant, you must go back and check whether each apparent main effect actually holds at *every* level of the other factor, or whether it's really just a simple effect in disguise.

### The 8-panel "which pattern is which" exercise (from slides)

The slides show 8 small panels, each combining three yes/no labels — presence/absence of a main effect of IV1 (~1 or 1), presence/absence of a main effect of IV2 (~2 or 2), and presence/absence of an interaction (~1×2 or 1×2) — with a matching line graph. The general reading strategy for any such panel:

1. **Check for a main effect of the x-axis variable (IV1):** do the points, averaged across both lines, shift up/down as you move from A to B?
2. **Check for a main effect of the line variable (IV2):** is one line consistently above the other, on average?
3. **Check for an interaction:** are the two lines parallel (no interaction) or not (interaction — converging, crossing, diverging, or one flat/one sloped)?
4. A panel can have **any combination** of these three yes/no answers — e.g., no main effects at all but a strong interaction (the two lines cross exactly in the middle, each with opposite slopes and equal means overall), or two main effects with **no** interaction (parallel, non-overlapping, sloped lines).

### Types of factorial designs (how participants are assigned) — from the textbook

Just as with one-IV designs (§19–21), a two-way (or higher) factorial design can assign participants to conditions in different ways:

| Type                                                             | Description                                                                                                                                   | Notes                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Completely randomized (independent samples) factorial design** | Participants are randomly assigned to **one** of all the treatment combinations (cells) — e.g., in a 2×2, four independent groups             | Analogous to a between-subjects design, extended to two factors                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| **Repeated measures (correlated samples) factorial design**      | **All** participants take part in **all** conditions/cells                                                                                    | Analogous to within-subjects design; reduces error from individual differences and needs fewer participants, but raises carryover-effect and counterbalancing concerns (as in §19). With 4 conditions, complete counterbalancing needs 24 different orders, which is often impractical — partial/simplified counterbalancing (each condition appears once in each ordinal position) is a common compromise |
| **Mixed design**                                                 | Participants are **randomly assigned** to the levels of **one** factor, but **repeatedly measured** across the levels of the **other** factor | Common when one factor is something you can't/shouldn't repeat within a person (e.g., a between-subjects "type of therapy" factor) crossed with something you can repeat (e.g., a within-subjects "time" factor, like the psychotherapy-length example above)                                                                                                                                              |

- A **2 × 3 Factorial Design** example from the slides plots "proportion correct" against "repetition" (1, 2, 3) with two lines for **delay** (immediate vs. one week) — repetition here is IV1 (3 levels), delay is IV2 (2 levels), giving a 3×2 design. Both lines rise with repetition (main effect of repetition) and the immediate-delay line sits consistently above the one-week line (main effect of delay); the lines are roughly parallel, so weak/no interaction.
- A further example turns this into a **2 × 2 × 2 design** by adding a third factor: modality (auditory vs. visual), shown as two side-by-side panels (facets) each containing the same repetition × delay lines. With three two-level factors, there are **three main effects** (repetition, modality, delay), **three possible two-way interactions** (repetition×modality, repetition×delay, modality×delay), and **one three-way interaction** (repetition×modality×delay) — 7 effects total, since a k-way factorial with all two-level factors has 2^k − 1 testable effects.
  - In the slide's worked version: there **is** a main effect of repetition (scores rise from rep 1 to rep 2) and **is** a main effect of modality (visual scores sit above auditory scores), but there is **no effect of delay** (the "Immediate" and "One week" lines from the earlier 2-way example collapse into a single line once modality is accounted for) — illustrating how adding a factor can reveal that an apparent main effect in a simpler design was actually confounded with, or explained away by, another variable.

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- A factorial design is still built from the same primitives as §19: IV, DV, controlled variables, extraneous variables — it just has **more than one IV**, and each IV can independently be run as within-subjects or between-subjects (§20's within/between distinction applies **separately to each factor**, which is exactly how a mixed design arises).
- The line-graph reading skill here (parallel vs. non-parallel lines) is the same graphical logic used throughout the slide deck's data-visualization examples and will recur in the sampling/statistics material.

## 23. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 8)

- **Factor:** an independent variable in a study with more than one IV.
- **Level:** one specific value/condition that a factor can take.
- **Factorial design:** a design with two or more IVs (factors), studied simultaneously, producing one condition (cell) for every combination of factor levels.
- **Total number of conditions:** the product of the number of levels of each factor (e.g., a 2×3 design has 6 conditions).
- **Main effect:** the effect of one factor on the DV, considered across/averaged over all levels of the other factor(s); graphically, a consistent overall shift as you move along one axis or between lines.
- **Interaction:** when the effect of one factor on the DV depends on the level of another factor; graphically, non-parallel lines (converging, crossing, or diverging).
- **Simple effect:** an apparent main effect that is actually only true at one level of another factor — i.e., an effect that is really a product of a significant interaction, not a genuine main effect.
- **Completely randomized (independent samples) factorial design:** participants randomly assigned to exactly one treatment combination.
- **Repeated-measures (correlated-samples) factorial design:** all participants experience all treatment combinations.
- **Mixed design:** participants are randomly assigned to levels of one factor but repeatedly measured across levels of another.
- **k-way interaction:** an interaction involving k factors simultaneously (e.g., a "three-way interaction" in a 2×2×2 design means the two-way interaction between two factors itself changes depending on the level of the third factor).

## 24. Data Taxonomy: Variables and Levels of Measurement

### What is a variable?

- A **variable** is simply **anything that varies** — temperature, height, anxiety level, dog breed, classification accuracy, automobile brand, which political party someone votes for, etc.
- The first major split in describing data is **qualitative vs. quantitative**:
  - **Qualitative data:** names, colors, smells — categories/labels, not numbers with inherent magnitude (e.g., "John," "Patricia," "green," "aromatic").
  - **Quantitative data:** scores, weights, shoe sizes — numbers that represent an amount.
  - Quantitative data further splits into **discrete** (countable, whole-number values — e.g., 5 of something) and **continuous** (can take any value in a range, limited only by precision of measurement — e.g., 3.265... units).

### The variable-type tree (Categorical vs. Numerical)

The slides organize "observed characteristics (variables)" into a tree:

```
Observed Characteristics (Variables)
├── Categorical Variables
│   ├── Nominal (e.g., hair color)
│   └── Ordinal (e.g., agreement: agree/neutral/disagree)
└── Measurement (Numerical) Variables
    ├── Discrete (e.g., number of siblings)
    └── Continuous (e.g., time since last meal)
```

#### Categorical variables

- **Definition:** discrete categories, or qualitative; a response can be placed in **one** category, not partway between categories.
- **Nominal:**
  - Can be numbers, letters, or anything — but they function purely as **labels**, with no inherent order or magnitude.
  - Examples: automobile brand, political party, a postal/PIN code, hair color.
  - **Dichotomous** is a special case of nominal with exactly **two** categories (e.g., pass/fail, thumbs up/thumbs down).
- **Ordinal:**
  - Indicates an **ordering/ranking** within a group, but **does not indicate the distance** between ranks.
  - Examples: clothing size (XS < S < M < L < XL — but the size difference between M→L is not necessarily equal to L→XL), spiciness level (Hot < Hotter < Hottest), agreement with a statement (agree/neutral/disagree), finishing position in a race (1st, 2nd, 3rd — we know who was faster, not by how much).

#### Numerical (measurement) variables

- **Definition:** variables that operate above the level of mere categorizing; typically quantitative. Examples: classification accuracy, age, number of courses taken.
- **Discrete:** a **whole numerical value**. Examples: number of courses taken this semester, number of word occurrences in a document, number of CPU cores. (Ice-cream analogy from the slides: "There are 3 cones," "Cone 1 has 2 scoops" — discrete counts.)
- **Continuous:** can take on an **unlimited number of values** between the lowest and highest points of measurement, limited only by the precision of the measuring instrument. Examples: height, weight, temperature. (Ice-cream analogy: "Cone 3 weighs 79.4 grams," "Cone 2 ice cream is at 8.3°F" — continuous measurements.)
  - Continuous variables split further into **interval** and **ratio** level (below).

### Levels of measurement (Stevens' typology: nominal → ordinal → interval → ratio)

This is the single most exam-relevant idea in this lecture — a **hierarchy of four "levels of measurement,"** each one adding a mathematical property the previous level lacked:

| Level        | Key property added                   | Can you rank order? | Are intervals equal/meaningful? | Is there a true (non-arbitrary) zero? | Examples                                             |
| ------------ | ------------------------------------ | ------------------- | ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Nominal**  | Categorization/labeling only         | No                  | No                              | No                                    | Nationality, favorite animal, hair color             |
| **Ordinal**  | Rank order                           | Yes                 | No                              | No                                    | "Bad, neutral, good"; class rank; finishing position |
| **Interval** | Equal-sized intervals between values | Yes                 | Yes                             | **No — zero is arbitrary**            | Year of birth; degrees Celsius/Fahrenheit            |
| **Ratio**    | True zero (absence of the quantity)  | Yes                 | Yes                             | **Yes — zero means "none"**           | Body length/height; weight; number of children; age  |

- **"Lower" levels can always be analyzed with methods valid at "higher" levels, but not vice versa.** In other words, the acceptable statistical toolkit **grows** as you move from nominal → ratio: nominal-level methods (e.g., counting frequencies, mode, chi-square) are always valid; ratio-level methods (e.g., means, ratios like "twice as much") require ratio data and are *not* valid to apply to merely nominal or ordinal data.
- **Interval vs. ratio, concretely:** temperature in Celsius is **interval** — 0°C does not mean "no temperature," so you cannot say 20°C is "twice as hot" as 10°C. Height in cm is **ratio** — 0 cm genuinely means "no height," so 180 cm really is twice as tall as 90 cm.
- **A worked height example from the slides** shows the same underlying data (8 people's heights in cm) represented at three different levels simultaneously:
  - **Interval scale:** the actual centimeter values (e.g., Simon = 180, YOU = ~172, ..., Adrian = 150) — gives real, measured differences.
  - **Ordinal scale:** only the rank (1st, 2nd, 3rd, ... 8th) — we know Simon is tallest and Adrian is shortest, but not that YOU are "a lot shorter" than Simon, just that you're 2nd.
  - **Nominal classification:** just "Tall" vs. "Short" — no distinction *at all* within the "Tall" group or within the "Short" group.
  - This illustrates that the **same raw phenomenon** can be recorded (or degraded) at different measurement levels, and each successive level throws away information the previous one had.
- **Continuous → categorical conversion:** a continuous variable (e.g., exact age) is often **binned into ordinal/categorical brackets** for analysis or reporting (e.g., 20–25 years = 1, 26–30 years = 2, ..., 46+ years = 6). This is a common, deliberate move from a higher level of measurement down to a lower one — usually done for ease of reporting or because finer distinctions aren't meaningful for the research question, but it does throw away precision.
- **At least interval-level data is needed to perform parametric statistical tests** (e.g., t-tests, Pearson's r, ANOVA — the tools you'll meet in later, stats-focused lectures).
- **In practice, ordinal data (e.g., Likert-scale ratings) are often treated as if they were interval,** and this is generally considered acceptable **if the data are roughly normally distributed** for that variable — this is a widely used, if debated, convention in behavioral research (and matches the broader academic debate — see Norman, 2010 — over whether Likert-type data can be legitimately analyzed with means/SDs despite being technically ordinal).

### Choosing a statistical test based on data type (preview / connective tissue to later stats lectures)

The slides include a decision-flow figure ("Choosing an appropriate two-sample test") that ties directly back to this taxonomy — you don't need to memorize the specific tests yet, but the **logic** is exam-relevant:

1. First ask: am I looking for a **difference** (between two conditions/groups) or a **correlation** (relationship between two variables)?
2. Then ask about **data type**: are the data **categorical** (frequencies), or are they **scores** — and if scores, do they meet **parametric assumptions** (roughly, are they interval/ratio and normally distributed) or not (making them more safely treated as ranked/ordinal)?
3. Then ask whether the **design is related** (repeated measures/within-subjects, §20) or **unrelated** (independent measures/between-subjects, §20).
4. This produces the correct test — e.g., **categorical + unrelated → Chi-square**; **parametric assumptions met + related → related-samples t-test**; **parametric assumptions not met + unrelated → Mann-Whitney U**; **ordinal correlation → Spearman's ρ**; **interval correlation → Pearson's r.**

A second figure in the slides (the "classical approach" flowchart) gives a compact **decision procedure for classifying a variable itself**:

1. Is the **order of data values undisputable**? → If **No**, it's **Nominal** (e.g., nationality, favorite animal).
2. If Yes — do the data values have a **fixed unit of measurement**? → If **No**, it's **Ordinal** (e.g., "bad, neutral, good").
3. If Yes — does a **zero value mean "nothing"**? → If **No**, it's **Interval** (e.g., year of birth, degrees Celsius); if **Yes**, it's **Ratio** (e.g., body length, number of children).

### Activity / worked examples (from slides)

- **"Your sister came top in all three maths tests, so she must be far better than everyone else"** — what to point out: a *rank* (ordinal information — "came top") tells you nothing about the **size of the gap** to the next person. She might have won by 40 points or by 1 point each time; ordinal data can't distinguish these. This is the practical payoff of understanding "ordinal ≠ interval": you cannot legitimately infer *magnitude* of superiority from rank alone.
- **"Three ways to measure driving ability"** (nominal / ordinal / interval-ratio):
  - Nominal: pass/fail on a driving test (a label with two categories — this is actually dichotomous nominal).
  - Ordinal: instructor's ranking of students from best to worst.
  - Interval/ratio: number of errors made, or time to complete a course, or a numeric score out of 100.
- **Ice-skating judges' marks for style/presentation** — safest to treat as **ordinal**, because although judges give numbers, the psychological/aesthetic distance between a 7.5 and 8.0 is not guaranteed to be the same as between 8.5 and 9.0 — the numbers function more like ranks of quality than truly equal-interval measurements (though in practice, as noted above, such data are sometimes analyzed as if interval).

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- This lecture is the **formal vocabulary** for something already implicit throughout the course: every DV you operationalize (§20) has to be measured at *some* level, and that level constrains which statistical tests (mentioned briefly here, covered in depth in later lectures) can legitimately be applied to it.
- The "levels of measurement" idea is a different axis from — but complementary to — the **within-subjects/between-subjects** and **experimental/quasi/non-experimental** distinctions from §19–21: those are about *how you collect* the data, while this lecture is about *what kind of numbers you end up with*.

## 25. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 9)

- **Qualitative data:** category/label data with no inherent numeric magnitude (names, colors, smells).
- **Quantitative data:** numeric data representing an amount; splits into discrete and continuous.
- **Categorical variable:** a variable whose values are discrete categories; a case belongs in one category, not between categories. Includes nominal and ordinal sub-types.
- **Numerical (measurement) variable:** a variable operating above the level of mere categorizing; includes discrete and continuous sub-types.
- **Nominal variable:** labels/categories with no inherent order (e.g., hair color, brand).
- **Dichotomous variable:** a nominal variable with exactly two categories.
- **Ordinal variable:** indicates rank/order but not the distance between ranks (e.g., agreement scale, clothing size).
- **Discrete variable:** a whole numerical value (a count).
- **Continuous variable:** can take any value (to any precision) within a range.
- **Interval level:** continuous data with equal intervals between values but an arbitrary zero point (e.g., °C).
- **Ratio level:** continuous data with equal intervals **and** a true, non-arbitrary zero point (e.g., height, weight).
- **Levels of measurement:** the nominal → ordinal → interval → ratio hierarchy; higher levels support all the statistical methods valid at lower levels, plus more.
- **Parametric statistical test:** a test (e.g., t-test, ANOVA, Pearson's r) that generally requires at least interval-level, (approximately) normally distributed data.

## 26. Sampling

### Population, sample, parameter, statistic

- **Population:** the entire, complete group that shares the characteristic(s) of interest to the researcher (e.g., "all people residing in the United States"). A single member of a population is called an **element**. If you collect data from **every** element of the population, that's a **census**, not a sample.
- **Sample:** a subset of elements selected from the population.
- We use **random selection** to go from population → sample, then use **inference** to go back from the sample to conclusions about the population.
- A **parameter** describes the population (conventionally denoted **μ**, the population mean) — usually unknown and unobservable in practice.
- A **statistic** describes the sample (conventionally denoted **x̄**, the sample mean) — this is what we actually calculate, and we use it to **estimate** the corresponding parameter.
- **Why sampling matters:** "garbage in, data out", but flawed — if the sampling process is flawed, no amount of careful statistical analysis afterward can fix the resulting conclusions. The whole logic of inferential statistics assumes the sample reasonably represents the population; if that assumption is false, conclusions about the population are not trustworthy, however rigorous the math looks.

### Sampling bias — why representativeness matters

- **Sampling bias** occurs when a sample **systematically** over- or under-represents parts of the population, so that conclusions drawn from the sample don't generalize to the full population, even though there may still be some randomness in *how* the (biased) sample was drawn.
- Slide examples:
  - **Crash-test dummies** historically modeled on the "average male" body, leading to safety standards that under-protect women — real-world consequence: for a female occupant, the odds of being seriously injured in a frontal crash are reported as **73% greater** than for a male occupant (controlling for age, height, BMI, collision severity, and vehicle model year), with specific body-region risk increases (head +22.1%, neck +44.7%, abdomen +38.5%, chest +26.4%, arm +58.2%, leg +79.7% higher risk than males).
  - **Pacemaker research** historically sampling only ~20% females — a sampling composition that doesn't reflect the population that will actually use the device.
  - **YouTube "controversy" study** that only samples *existing* YouTube videos: conclusions about "YouTube controversy" would only generalize to the sampled **subset of videos actually on the platform**, not to the platform (or to content that never got uploaded/promoted) as a whole.
  - **W.E.I.R.D. research** (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic): from 2003–2007, 93% of studies in top-6 psychology journals used only Westerners as test subjects, with 68% of all studies drawing on U.S. samples alone — despite the U.S. representing only about 5% of the world's population and Western countries together representing roughly 12% of the world's population. This is a systemic sampling bias across the *entire field*, not just individual studies, and matters because findings from WEIRD samples may not generalize to non-WEIRD populations.
  - **"Who is missing?" exercises** from the slides: surveying café patrons 9–11 PM about public-space use misses people who don't go to cafés, parents/caregivers at home, people who feel unsafe traveling at night, older adults, people with disabilities, and people with different work schedules. Recruiting only from a university gym to study motivational music and exercise persistence misses people who don't exercise regularly, who exercise outdoors/at home, who can't afford/access a gym, older/non-university populations, people with physical limitations, and people who dislike exercise. **The general skill being tested here: for any given sampling frame, ask who systematically could never end up in that sample, and whether that excluded group differs on the variable you care about.**

### Probability vs. non-probability sampling

|                          | **Probability sampling**                                                                                      | **Non-probability sampling**                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Definition               | All individuals/items have a **known (or calculable) probability** of belonging to the sample                 | Odds of any member being selected **cannot be calculated**                                                                      |
| Also called              | Random / representative sampling                                                                              | —                                                                                                                               |
| Generalizability         | Considered the best means of obtaining a representative sample; supports rigorous inference to the population | Representativeness/generalizability is uncertain — the population the sample "belongs to" often can't even be clearly specified |
| Cost/effort              | Generally more difficult and expensive                                                                        | Generally quicker, cheaper, more convenient                                                                                     |
| **Key textbook caution** | —                                                                                                             | —                                                                                                                               |

- **Important caveat repeated in both slides and textbook: a sample can still be biased even though there is some randomness in how it was selected** — randomness in the *selection mechanism* is not the same thing as *representativeness* of the result. (E.g., randomly calling landline phone numbers is "random" but is not representative of the population once most people no longer have landlines.)
- **Random sampling ≠ random assignment.** Random *sampling* is about how you select participants from a population (this chapter); random *assignment* is about how you place already-selected participants into experimental conditions/groups (§19–21). These are separate concerns and one does not guarantee the other.

### Probability sampling techniques

| Technique                        | How it works                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                           | Pros                                                                                                               | Cons                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Simple random sampling (SRS)** | Every member of the population has an **equal** probability of being sampled (e.g., via a random-number table, computer-generated random selection, or drawing well-shuffled names from a container)                                                                                                                                                   | Easily understood; results are statistically projectable to the population                                         | Requires a complete, accurate, up-to-date list of the entire population (a "sampling frame") — very difficult/time-consuming/expensive to construct for large populations; no *guarantee* of a perfectly representative sample on any given draw          |
| **Stratified (random) sampling** | Population is divided into subgroups ("strata") based on an important characteristic (e.g., income bracket, sex, university), then a **random** sample is drawn from **each stratum** — either in proportion to each stratum's size in the population (for representativeness) or in equal numbers per stratum (to optimize between-group comparisons) | Highly representative and generalizable; ensures small subgroups aren't missed or under/over-represented by chance | Extremely time-consuming; needs a complete list of the population **within each stratum**; there's a practical limit to how many strata you can use                                                                                                       |
| **Systematic sampling**          | Participants are selected at a **regular interval** from an ordered list (e.g., every 5th house, every 10th name on a register)                                                                                                                                                                                                                        | Avoids clustered/haphazard selection; assures the population is sampled evenly across the list                     | Can coincide with **hidden periodicity** in the list — if the interval happens to line up with some repeating pattern in how the list is ordered, the sample can become badly biased (e.g., sampling every 7th day of data and always landing on Sundays) |
| **Cluster sampling**             | Population is divided into **naturally occurring groups** ("clusters," e.g., schools, city blocks, office floors); the researcher **randomly selects whole clusters** rather than selecting individuals directly, then studies everyone (or a further sample) within the chosen clusters                                                               | High feasibility — can be conducted efficiently in groups/by proximity, reducing cost                              | Least representative of the probability techniques; can skew results due to **within-cluster similarity** (people in the same cluster tend to be more alike than the population at large, e.g. same office floor → similar job roles)                     |
| **Multi-stage sampling**         | Combines several of the above techniques in **stages** — e.g., first cluster-sample regions, then stratify within each selected region, then simple-random-sample individuals within each stratum                                                                                                                                                      | Practical way to sample very large/dispersed populations without needing one master list of every individual       | More complex to design and analyze; each stage introduces its own potential source of sampling error                                                                                                                                                      |

**Stratified vs. Cluster sampling — key differences (slide table, source: Malhotra & Birks-style comparison):**

|                                  | Stratified Sampling                                                                                    | Cluster Sampling                                                                                                |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Meaning                          | Population divided into **homogeneous** segments (strata); sample randomly taken **from** the segments | Population's naturally-occurring **clusters** are the sampling unit; clusters themselves are selected at random |
| Sample composed of               | Randomly selected **individuals**, taken from all strata                                               | **All** individuals from the randomly selected clusters                                                         |
| Selection of population elements | Individually                                                                                           | Collectively (as intact groups)                                                                                 |
| Homogeneity                      | **Within** group (each stratum is internally similar on the stratifying variable)                      | **Between** groups (clusters resemble each other; it's within a cluster that you get diversity, ideally)        |
| Heterogeneity                    | **Between** groups (strata differ from each other on the stratifying variable)                         | **Within** group (ideally each cluster is a "mini" version of the whole population)                             |
| Who imposes the divisions        | The researcher (bifurcation imposed)                                                                   | Naturally occurring                                                                                             |
| Objective                        | Increase **precision** and representation                                                              | Reduce **cost** and improve efficiency                                                                          |

- **From the textbook (Ch. 7) — the 4 steps of simple random sampling**, useful as an exam checklist for describing *how* you would actually carry out random sampling for a given study:
  1. **Define the population** — precisely state who/what you want to generalize to (e.g., "all children aged 5–15 in the U.S." vs. narrower, more feasible definitions like "5- to 15-year-olds in a specific school district"). Narrowing the population makes sampling more feasible but also narrows what you can generalize to.
  2. **Construct a list** — obtain a complete, exhaustive, up-to-date listing of every population member (a sampling frame). Available ready-made lists (voter rolls, professional directories, school registers) are convenient but may be incomplete, outdated, or systematically miss certain groups (e.g., a homeowner list excludes renters/transients).
  3. **Draw the sample** — using a genuinely random method (random-number tables, computer-generated random selection). Historically: dice, coin flips, drawing names from a drum.
  4. **Contact members of the sample** — and here representativeness can be lost again: if only some of the selected people actually respond/participate (e.g., 80 out of 100 surveys returned), the achieved sample may no longer represent the population as well as the originally *drawn* sample did, especially if non-responders differ systematically from responders. **Historical cautionary examples:** the 1936 *Literary Digest* poll ("Landon by a Landslide") wrongly predicted the winner over Roosevelt due to a biased sampling frame (telephone/car-registration lists over-represented wealthier Americans); the 1948 "Dewey Defeats Truman" headline was based on early/incomplete polling that failed to anticipate how undecided voters would ultimately vote.

### Non-probability sampling techniques

| Technique                                                                | How it works                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                       | Pros                                                                                                                                                                       | Cons                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Convenience sampling**                                                 | Using whoever is easiest for the researcher to access at the time (e.g., people passing by, students who volunteer for a study)                                                                                                                                                    | Easier and cheaper; requires little planning; especially useful for **pilot studies**                                                                                      | Bias; the population the sample was drawn from often **cannot even be specified** ("a sample in search of a population"), so results are risky to generalize                                                                                  |
| **Purposive sampling** (also called **judgment/expert-choice sampling**) | The researcher deliberately selects participants believed to be most representative of the issue, or who have relevant expertise                                                                                                                                                   | Facilitates the specific purpose of the study; low-cost, ideal for **exploratory research**                                                                                | Biased and has **low generalizability**; selection is subjective                                                                                                                                                                              |
| **Quota sampling**                                                       | The researcher sets target numbers ("quotas") for subgroups based on their proportion in the population (e.g., exactly 50 freshmen, 50 sophomores, 50 juniors, 50 seniors), then fills each quota with whoever is available/convenient (no random selection **within** each quota) | Moderate cost, extensively used (e.g., by the Gallup Poll for political predictions); forces the sample to **mirror key population demographics** on the chosen dimensions | Selection **within** quotas isn't random, so there's no assurance of representativeness beyond the quota'd dimensions; not necessarily exhaustive of all relevant categories; may completely miss internal diversity within each quota bucket |
| **Referral / snowball sampling**                                         | The researcher recruits an initial small set of participants and asks them to refer other potential participants (who refer further participants, etc.)                                                                                                                            | Useful when the population is completely unknown or hard to access directly (e.g., studying a hidden/hard-to-reach population, or people with a rare characteristic)       | Bias due to **focused/interconnected social groups** (referred participants tend to resemble the people who referred them), and hence low generalizability; can be time-consuming                                                             |

| ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

- **Additional "which technique?" prompts from the slides (identifying the target population is part of the exercise):**
  1. Picking anybody off the street to answer a questionnaire (target: general public) → **not a random sample** — this is convenience sampling; people on a given street/at a given time are not representative of "the general public."
  2. Selecting every fifth home in a street (target: people living in the street) → **systematic sampling of that specific street's population** — this *can* be considered a valid random-type technique for that narrowly defined population, though not for any broader population.
  3. Selecting every tenth name on the school register (target: all pupils) → **systematic sampling**, valid for generalizing to that school's pupils (assuming no hidden periodicity in the register's ordering).
  4. Sticking a pin in a list of names (target: the names on the list) → functionally similar to simple random selection **if** it's genuinely haphazard/unbiased, though in practice people's pin placement is rarely truly random.
  5. - **"Can cheating make your marriage stronger?" (textbook Thinking-Critically box):** a sensationalized "study" claiming infidelity strengthens marriages almost certainly relied on a **self-selected or convenience sample** (e.g., volunteers willing to discuss their affairs) rather than any probability method — illustrating how weak sampling methodology can produce a headline-grabbing but meaningless/unrepresentative conclusion.

### Sample size, effect size, and statistical power (bridge into upcoming stats content)

- **Sample size for quantitative studies depends on the anticipated effect size.** Effect size = a quantitative measure of the magnitude of the experimental effect.
  - **Large effects → fewer participants needed** to detect them reliably.
  - **Small effects → more participants needed.**
  - E.g., if you expect motivational music to have a very large effect on exercise persistence, you may need fewer participants than if you expect only a small effect.
- **Statistical power** = the probability of **detecting an effect if a real effect actually exists** = **1 − β**, where β is the probability of a **false negative (Type II error)** — failing to detect a real effect.
  - **Larger sample sizes increase statistical power.**
- **A priori power analysis** — done *before* data collection, to decide how many participants are needed — requires three inputs:
  1. **Effect size** (often estimated from previous/pilot studies).
  2. **Significance level** (α, conventionally 0.05 or lower — this connects to §17's significance-testing material).
  3. **Desired power** (1 − β), conventionally **greater than 0.8** (i.e., at least an 80% chance of detecting the effect if it's really there).
- **From the textbook — sampling error:** even with a perfectly executed random sampling procedure, some **sampling error** is inevitable, because a sample will almost never *exactly* reproduce the population's characteristics.
  - **Systematic sampling error:** the fault of the investigator/design — the sample is biased in a consistent direction because it wasn't drawn properly (e.g., the *Literary Digest* poll), or because people were dropped from the sample non-randomly (e.g., only the more motivated/available people responded).
  - **Random sampling error (chance error):** unavoidable, non-directional variation between samples drawn from the same population purely by chance (e.g., flipping 100 coins won't give exactly 50 heads every time). This kind of error **can be quantified statistically** (e.g., "95% confidence that the true proportion lies between 43% and 47%" — a **confidence interval**), unlike systematic error, which cannot simply be modeled away.
  - **Larger samples reduce (average) random sampling error** — but there are diminishing returns: beyond a certain sample size, the cost/effort of further reducing error becomes disproportionately large relative to the benefit. (Textbook example: the Nielsen ratings organization surveys only ~13,000 people to estimate the TV-viewing habits of ~250 million Americans — about 1 in every 20,000 viewers — and this is considered sufficiently accurate for its purpose, showing that a well-selected small sample can still be highly informative.)
  - **Population variability also matters:** the more diverse the population is on the characteristic of interest, and the more factors of interest there are, the larger a sample must be to remain representative (e.g., if everyone in a population were exactly the same height, a sample of size 1 would be perfectly representative; if heights ranged from 4 to 7 feet, a much larger sample would be needed).

### Evaluating sample-based information as a critical consumer

- Polls/surveys conducted via magazines, radio call-ins, internet click-polls, and pay-per-call TV numbers are **not** based on random sampling — respondents **self-select**, meaning the resulting sample typically represents only a narrow, self-selected segment of people who (a) had access to the poll, (b) chose to participate, and (c) had time/interest to respond. Reputable sources (e.g., CNN's online "QuickVote") explicitly disclaim this ("not scientific... cannot be assumed to represent the opinions of Internet users in general").
- The general critical-thinking questions to ask about any reported sample-based finding: **What is the actual population being generalized to? How was the sample drawn (probability or non-probability)? What was the response/completion rate, and could non-responders differ systematically from responders?**

### How this connects back to earlier sections

- Sampling technique determines **external/ecological validity** (§21) — how well a study's findings generalize beyond the specific sample — while random *assignment* (a separate concept, see caveat above) is what supports **internal validity** and causal claims (§21) once participants are already in the study.
- The **WEIRD** critique is a real-world, field-wide instance of the **sampling bias** concept applied not just within one study but across the discipline's evidence base — worth connecting back to the external-validity discussion in §21 and to the qualitative/quantitative and field-vs-lab tradeoffs discussed earlier in the course (§9, §21).
- Effect size, α, and power tie directly forward to the **significance testing** material in §17 — sampling and statistical inference are two ends of the same pipeline: **how you got your data** (this lecture) determines how much you're entitled to conclude from **what the data show** (§17).

## 27. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 10)

- **Population:** the entire group sharing the characteristic(s) of interest; a single member is an **element**.
- **Census:** data obtained from **every** member of a population (as opposed to a sample).
- **Sample:** a subset of population elements selected for study.
- **Parameter (μ):** a numerical characteristic of a population (usually unknown).
- **Statistic (x̄):** a numerical characteristic of a sample, used to estimate the corresponding parameter.
- **Sampling bias:** systematic over/under-representation of parts of the population in a sample, undermining generalizability.
- **W.E.I.R.D. research:** the tendency for psychological research to over-rely on samples from Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic populations, limiting generalizability to humanity as a whole.
- **Probability sampling:** every population element has a known/calculable chance of selection; supports rigorous generalization.
- **Non-probability sampling:** the chance of any element's selection cannot be calculated; generalization is uncertain.
- **Simple random sampling (SRS):** every population member has an equal chance of selection.
- **Stratified (random) sampling:** population divided into strata (homogeneous subgroups); random samples drawn from each stratum.
- **Systematic sampling:** participants selected at a fixed interval from an ordered list; risk of hidden periodicity.
- **Cluster sampling:** naturally occurring groups (clusters) are randomly selected as whole units; risk of within-cluster similarity reducing representativeness.
- **Multi-stage sampling:** combines multiple sampling techniques across sequential stages.
- **Convenience sampling:** using whoever is easiest to access; fast/cheap but high bias risk; useful for pilot studies.
- **Purposive (judgment/expert-choice) sampling:** researcher deliberately selects participants believed most relevant/expert; low generalizability.
- **Quota sampling:** target numbers set for subgroups to mirror population proportions; selection within quotas is not random.
- **Referral (snowball) sampling:** initial participants refer further participants; useful for hard-to-reach/unknown populations.
- **Sampling frame:** the actual list of the population used to draw a sample; must be exhaustive and up to date.
- **Random sampling (as distinct from random assignment):** the process of selecting participants from a population, as opposed to random *assignment*, which places already-selected participants into experimental conditions.
- **Sampling error:** the degree to which a sample fails to perfectly represent its population; splits into systematic and random sampling error.
- **Systematic sampling error:** bias introduced by flawed sampling procedure or non-random loss of participants — the investigator's "fault."
- **Random sampling error (chance error):** unavoidable, non-directional variability between samples from the same population; quantifiable via confidence intervals.
- **Confidence interval:** a range, with an associated probability (e.g., 95%), within which the true population value is expected to fall.
- **Effect size:** a quantitative measure of the magnitude of an experimental effect; larger effects require smaller samples to detect reliably, and vice versa.
- **Statistical power (1 − β):** the probability of detecting a real effect if one exists; increased by larger sample sizes.
- **Type II error (β):** a false negative — failing to detect a real effect.
- **A priori power analysis:** a pre-data-collection calculation of required sample size, based on expected effect size, chosen significance level, and desired power.

## 28. Randomized Control Trials (RCT) — Lecture 11

An **RCT** is a study design in which **individuals are allocated at random to receive one of several interventions** (at least two total): typically an **intervention (treatment) arm** and a **control (placebo) arm**.

### Core defining features
- **RCTs are experimental** — the intervention is *controlled by the investigator* (this is what separates them from purely observational/correlational designs).
- **RCTs are usually comparative studies** — at least two arms are compared against each other.
- **Random allocation** means every participant has a *defined, known probability* of being assigned to a particular intervention.
  - Allocation is **not determined** by the investigator, the clinicians, or the participants themselves.
  - Allocation is **not predictable** based on any pattern (this is why proper randomization — not just "alternating" patients — matters).

### Why use an RCT?
1. **Minimizes bias in treatment assignment.** All factors — known *and unknown* — that could affect the outcome end up (on average) equally distributed across the study groups, because assignment is random rather than based on any characteristic of the participant.
2. **Facilitates blinding (masking)** of the identity of the treatment from the investigator, the participants, and/or the assessors — including the possible use of a **placebo**. (Blinding is what randomization *enables*; they are related but distinct concepts — randomization is about *assignment*, blinding is about *concealing which arm someone is in*.)
3. **Permits the use of probability theory** to assess whether observed differences between treatment groups are likely to have arisen merely by chance (this is the bridge into the **significance testing** material from §17).
4. **Gives a chance to establish causality** — because the investigator actively manipulates the independent variable (which arm a participant is in) and random assignment rules out systematic pre-existing differences between groups, an RCT is one of the strongest designs for supporting a causal claim (echoing the "control" goal of science and the internal-validity logic covered in §21 and again in §39 below).

### Classic examples cited in the lecture
- **Effect of Deworming on Malnourished Preschool Children in India** (Awasthi et al., 2008, *PLoS Neglected Tropical Diseases*)
- **RCT of Open versus Laparoscopic Appendectomy**

Both examples are medical/clinical RCTs, underscoring that RCT logic originated in — and is still a gold standard within — clinical/medical research, but the same logic (random assignment + comparison arms + blinding where possible) transfers directly to behavioral and psychological experiments (e.g., randomly assigning participants to a "music intervention" vs. a "no-music control" condition).

## 29. Parametric Designs & Psychometric Functions — Lecture 11

### Parametric designs
A **parametric design** lets you look at the dependent variable (DV) as you **systematically vary the independent variable (IV)** — i.e., you vary the *levels* of the IV in a semi-continuous (graded, stepwise) manner, rather than just comparing two or three discrete conditions.

A classic domain where parametric designs show up is **perceptual processing of sensory signals** — for example:
- **Psychometric functions** in general
- **Sensitivity of hearing** (what an audiologist measures)
- **Eye power / visual acuity testing** (what an optometrist measures)

In all these cases, the experimenter presents a range of graded stimulus intensities (loudness, letter size, contrast, etc.) and records how the participant's *response* changes as a function of that intensity.

### Psychometric functions
A **function**, generally, is a rule for turning one number into another number. In a **psychometric function**, you take **one number** (a *quantified stimulus* — a physical quantity) and turn it into **another number** (the *probability of a particular behavioral response*).

- **By convention:**
  - The **physical quantity** (the stimulus) goes on the **abscissa** (x-axis) — this is the "physics" axis.
  - The **behavioral response** (proportion of chosen response, 0 to 1) goes on the **ordinate** (y-axis) — this is the "psycho" (psychophysics) axis.
- This is the foundational idea behind **psychophysics** and **Signal Detection Theory**.

### Reading a psychometric function: two key features

**1. Slope → Discriminability (Sensitivity)**
The **steepness of the curve's rise** indicates how well the observer can *discriminate* between different stimulus values.
- A **step function** (near-vertical rise) = very high discriminability — the observer sharply switches their response as soon as the stimulus crosses a threshold value.
- A **flat function** (horizontal line, e.g., staying at 0.5 across all stimulus values) = **no discriminability at all** — the observer's responses don't track the stimulus; they're responding at chance regardless of what's presented.
- An **intermediate slope** = intermediate/graded discriminability — a more gradual, S-shaped rise. Most real psychophysical data look like this rather than a perfect step function.

**2. X-intercept (specifically, the stimulus value at the 50% response point) → Bias**
Psychometric functions can differ from each other in **where along the x-axis** they cross the 50% response mark — this is an index of **bias**, and specifically of the **Point of Subjective Equality (PSE)**.
- **PSE** = the abscissa value (the stimulus quantity) associated with the **50% ordinate value** (the point where the participant is equally likely to give either response).
- To the extent that the x-intercept (PSE) departs from the **center of the range of stimuli being tested**, there is **bias**.
  - **Important terminological note from the slides:** this "bias" refers to the *centre value on the psychometric function shifting away from the true/objective midpoint* — it does **not** refer to human cognitive/social bias in the colloquial sense. The lecture explicitly flags this distinction ("Bias-sensitivity").
- **No bias**: the curve's 50%-point sits exactly at the center of the stimulus range.
- **Liberal bias**: the curve is shifted so that the 50%-point occurs at a *lower* (more negative) stimulus value than the true center — the observer is more willing to give the "yes"/positive response even for weaker stimuli.
- **Conservative bias**: the curve is shifted so that the 50%-point occurs at a *higher* stimulus value than the true center — the observer requires more evidence/stronger stimuli before giving the "yes"/positive response.

### Why this matters for the course
Psychometric functions are a bridge concept: they show how a *parametric* experimental design (systematically varying stimulus intensity) combined with *repeated measurement* of a behavioral response lets you extract **two independent, meaningful summary parameters** — a **sensitivity** measure (slope) and a **bias** measure (location) — from what would otherwise just look like a pile of raw proportions. This same "separate sensitivity from bias" logic reappears throughout psychophysics and signal-detection-theory-based work (e.g., d′ and criterion in SDT), even though the lecture doesn't derive d′ explicitly.

## 30. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 11)

- **Randomized Control Trial (RCT):** an experimental, comparative study design in which participants are randomly allocated to one of at least two intervention arms (treatment vs. control/placebo), with allocation probability defined and unpredictable, and not determined by investigator/clinician/participant.
- **Intervention (treatment) arm:** the study group that receives the active treatment/manipulation under investigation.
- **Control (placebo) arm:** the comparison study group, which may receive no treatment, a standard treatment, or an inert placebo.
- **Blinding (masking):** concealing which arm/treatment a participant, investigator, or assessor has been assigned to, in order to reduce bias.
- **Parametric design:** an experimental design in which the DV is examined as the IV is systematically varied across a semi-continuous range of levels.
- **Psychometric function:** a function mapping a quantified physical stimulus (x-axis/abscissa) to the probability of a behavioral response (y-axis/ordinate).
- **Abscissa:** the horizontal (x) axis of a graph; in a psychometric function, represents the physical stimulus quantity.
- **Ordinate:** the vertical (y) axis of a graph; in a psychometric function, represents the proportion of the chosen behavioral response.
- **Discriminability (sensitivity):** the degree to which an observer's responses distinguish between different stimulus values; visually read from the **slope** of a psychometric function (steeper = more discriminable).
- **Step function:** a psychometric function with a near-vertical rise, indicating very high discriminability / a sharp threshold.
- **Flat function:** a psychometric function with no meaningful slope (response probability stays constant, e.g., near 0.5, regardless of stimulus value), indicating no discriminability.
- **Point of Subjective Equality (PSE):** the stimulus value (x-intercept) at which the response probability equals 50%; used as an index of bias.
- **Bias (in a psychometric function):** the degree to which the PSE departs from the true center of the tested stimulus range — distinct from everyday/social "bias."
- **Liberal bias:** the psychometric function's 50%-point is shifted toward lower stimulus values than the true center (more readiness to give the positive response).
- **Conservative bias:** the psychometric function's 50%-point is shifted toward higher stimulus values than the true center (less readiness to give the positive response; more evidence required).
- **Signal Detection Theory:** the broader psychophysical framework within which psychometric functions and the sensitivity/bias distinction are situated.

## 31. Types of Statistics: Descriptive vs. Inferential — Lecture 12

**Statistics**, as a field, branches into:

| Branch | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Descriptive statistics** | **Organizing, summarizing, and describing** the data you actually collected. Presents the characteristics of the data itself — measures of central tendency and dispersion. |
| **Inferential statistics** | **Generalizing** from a sample to a population; **testing hypotheses** (e.g., group differences) and making predictions. |

A related pairing shown in the lecture's flow diagram: **Statistics → Correlational → Relationships → Significance**, and **Statistics → Inferential → Generalising**. That is, correlational analysis (looking at relationships among variables) feeds into significance testing, which is one branch of inferential statistics — descriptive statistics is the separate, prior step of organizing/summarizing/describing the raw data before any inference is attempted.

**Descriptive statistics** = "described data." **Inferential statistics** = "studies a sample [in order to draw conclusions about] the same [wider] data" (i.e., the population).

This directly parallels — and is the natural continuation of — the earlier course distinction (§26, sampling) between a **sample statistic** and a **population parameter**: descriptive stats characterize what you *have* (the sample); inferential stats let you say something about what you *don't have direct access to* (the population), using significance testing (§17) as the main tool.

## 32. Measures of Central Tendency — Lecture 12

**Central tendency** = the **most typical value** of a given group of values. The three common measures:

- **Mean:** the arithmetic average.
- **Median:** the middle value when data are ordered.
- **Mode:** the most frequently occurring value.

*(Worked mini-example from the slides — heights 155, 155, 158, 164, 168: Mode = 155 (appears twice), Median = 158 (the middle value), Mean = 160.)*

### Which measure fits which variable type?
Recall the **scales of measurement** from §24 (nominal, ordinal, interval, ratio). Central tendency measures aren't all valid for all scales:

| Variable type | Example | Central tendency measure(s) |
|---|---|---|
| **Nominal** (categorical, unordered) | hair color | **Mode** only |
| **Ordinal** (categorical, ordered) | agreement with a tax proposal (agree/neutral/disagree) | **Median, Mode** |
| **Discrete** (measurement/numerical, countable) | number of siblings | **Mean, Mode** |
| **Continuous** (measurement/numerical, uncountable range) | time since last meal | **Mean, Median, Mode** (all technically usable, mean typically preferred) |

This table is essentially the "Central Tendency for Variable Types" diagram: categorical variables split into nominal (mode only) and ordinal (median/mode); measurement variables split into discrete (mean/mode) and continuous (mean/median/mode).

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Measure | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | A sensitive and exact measure of the centre point of a group of values | A single extreme value in one direction can seriously distort the mean |
| **Median** | Not as susceptible to extreme values as the mean | Can be unrepresentative if there are only a small number of values |
| **Mode** | Indicates the most important/common value; unaffected by extreme scores; more informative than the mean in some contexts | Not useful for small data sets where several values occur equally frequently |

**Practical takeaway:** when a distribution is skewed or has outliers (see §34 below), the **median** is usually the more representative measure of central tendency, since the **mean** is pulled toward the extreme values.

## 33. Measures of Dispersion/Spread — Lecture 12

**Dispersion (spread)** = how much all the other values in the group vary around the typical (central-tendency) value.

### The four measures covered

1. **Range** = maximum value − minimum value (e.g., for {3,4,6,7,9}: Range = 9 − 3 = 6).
2. **Interquartile Range (IQR)** = the range of the *middle 50%* of the data, from the **25th percentile (Q1)** to the **75th percentile (Q3)**; visualized as the box in a boxplot, with the **median (Q2)** marked inside it.
3. **Variance (σ²)** = <code>Σ(x − μ)² / N</code> — the average of the squared deviations from the mean.
4. **Standard Deviation (SD)** = <code>√(Σ|x − x̄|² / n)</code> — the square root of the variance; expressed in the same units as the original data (unlike variance, which is in squared units), which is why SD is more commonly reported/interpreted directly.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Measure | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|---|
| **Range** | Fine to use for *all* scale types **except Nominal** | Distorted by extreme values; gives no indication of how data are grouped/clustered around the mean |
| **Variance / SD** | Fundamental to significance testing, and forms the basis of **Analysis of Variance (ANOVA)**; enables population parameters to be estimated from a sample | Appropriate **only** for **Interval and Ratio** scale data |

This mirrors the central-tendency table above: dispersion measures are similarly scale-dependent, and variance/SD in particular require data where the *amount* of difference is meaningful (interval/ratio scales — see §24), not just direction or category membership.

## 34. The Normal Distribution & Z-Scores — Lecture 12

### What is the Normal Distribution?
A **bell-shaped mathematical curve** describing how values are distributed.
- Data taken from a sample is **assumed** to be "normally distributed," and must **approximate this shape** in order to use **parametric tests of significance**.
- **Inferential statistics** such as **t-tests, F-tests, and regression analyses** require (in some sense) that the numeric variables involved are **approximately normally distributed**.
- **Note:** the normal distribution does **not** fit all populations — normality is an assumption to be checked, not a given.

### Key properties
- **Symmetrical** about the horizontal-axis midpoint.
- **Mean, median, and mode all fall on the same midpoint.**
- No matter what the mean (μ) and standard deviation (σ) are, the proportion of data falling within a given number of SDs of the mean is fixed:
  - **μ − σ to μ + σ ≈ 68%** of the data (**"1 SD" / 68% rule**)
  - **μ − 2σ to μ + 2σ ≈ 95%** of the data
  - **μ − 3σ to μ + 3σ ≈ 99.7%** of the data
  - Almost **all** values fall within **3 standard deviations** of the mean.

### Standardization and the Z-score
Any normal distribution can be **standardized** into the **Standard Normal Distribution** (mean = 0, SD = 1) using the **z-score formula**:

**Z = (x − μ) / σ**

Where: x = the individual score, μ = the mean, σ = the standard deviation.

Standardizing serves two purposes (from the slides' worked "Quiz 1, Spring 2017" example):
a) **Allows calculation of the probability of a score occurring** within the normal distribution.
b) **Enables comparison of two scores from different normal distributions** — e.g., comparing Quiz 1 scores from Spring 2017 to Quiz 1 scores from Spring 2018, which may have different means/SDs, by putting both on the same (standardized) scale.

## 35. Skewness & Distribution Shapes — Lecture 12

Mean, median, mode, and range/SD can **fail to be representative** of the data when the distribution departs from normality — most commonly, when it is **skewed**.

### Skewed distributions
A skewed distribution resembles something closer to an exponential distribution: it has **lots of extreme values far from the mean or mode**, and it is **not straightforward to run useful statistical tests** on this type of distribution (without transformation or non-parametric alternatives).

| Skew type | Shape / ordering of mean-median-mode | Cause | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Negative skew** (left-skewed) | Long tail to the left; **Mean < Median < Mode** | Results from relatively **easy tasks**, due to a **ceiling effect** (most people cluster near the top/max possible score) | Scores on an **easy quiz** |
| **Normal (no skew)** | Symmetric; **Mean = Median = Mode** | — | — |
| **Positive skew** (right-skewed) | Long tail to the right; **Mode < Median < Mean** | Results from tasks that are **hard to improve upon**, due to a **floor effect** | **Reaction time (RT)** data; scores on a **difficult quiz** |

Additional real-world examples cited: "the age at which married men die" (a classically skewed distribution used as a textbook illustration).

### Bimodal distributions
- **Two distinct peaks** in the distribution.
- A **probable indicator of (at least) two underlying groups** in the data, rather than one homogeneous population.
- Example: **completion time of marathon runners** (competitive/trained runners cluster around one peak, casual/recreational runners around another).

### The Boxplot as a distribution-shape diagnostic
A **boxplot** (box-and-whisker plot) displays: **Minimum**, **Q1 (25th percentile)**, **Median (Q2)**, **Q3 (75th percentile)**, and **Maximum**, with the **IQR** as the box and **potential outliers** flagged separately (points beyond **Q1 − 1.5×IQR** or **Q3 + 1.5×IQR**).
- **Uniform distribution:** a wide, evenly-spread box with short/no whiskers relative to its width, and Q1/Median/Q3 roughly evenly spaced.
- **Bell-shaped (normal) distribution:** median centered in the box, whiskers roughly symmetric.
- **Skewed distributions:** the box and whiskers become asymmetric, with the median shifted off-center within the box and one whisker noticeably longer than the other — this is exactly how a boxplot lets you *read off* skew at a glance, tying directly back to the "why do we visualize" motivation in §36.

## 36. Data Visualization — Lecture 12

### Why do we visualize?
Visualization serves several distinct research functions, not just "making results pretty":
1. **Allows for initial guesses of data distribution** (e.g., is it approximately normal?)
2. **Direction of effect** — quick visual read on which group/condition is higher or lower.
3. **Error detection** (e.g., spotting missing values, NaNs, impossible values).
4. **Outlier detection.**
5. **Presenting results** (the final, communicative function, as opposed to the exploratory functions 1–4 above).

This maps onto a broader **two-phase framing** the lecture draws explicitly: **initial/exploratory data visualization** (used by the researcher themselves, to sanity-check and explore) versus **final results visualization** (used to communicate findings to an audience). Plots like histograms, boxplots, and scatter plots serve the first purpose especially well because they reveal **bin-width dependency** (histograms), quartile structure and **outliers** (boxplots), and **relationships between two variables** (scatter plots) — all useful *before* you've committed to a specific statistical model.

### What makes a good visualization?
- **Reduce cognitive load:**
  - **Simplicity**
  - **Relevancy**
  - **Less is more** — this is the **data-ink ratio** principle: *"Remove to improve"* (attributed to Darkhorse Analytics' "Data Looks Better Naked" — every non-data pixel (unnecessary gridlines, 3D effects, heavy borders, redundant labels) should be scrutinized and removed unless it adds real information).
- **Storytelling:**
  - Ability to **support the reader** during their interpretive "journey" through the graphic.
  - **Convince the reader** — a good visualization doesn't just display data, it makes an argument.
- Quoted design philosophy: *"Perfection is achieved not when there is nothing more to add, but when there is nothing left to take away."* — Antoine de Saint-Exupéry.

### Bad designs and misleading graphs
The lecture uses the UK's Brexit "Should Britain leave the EU?" poll graphic (43% Leave vs. 39% Remain) as a worked example of **accurate-but-misleading** graphics:
1. **Truncated/non-zero baseline:** if the y-axis doesn't start at 0, small real differences can be made to look dramatically larger than they are. *Fix: baseline should start at zero, not at some arbitrary higher value* (the slide's example: baseline at 37 instead of 0 visually exaggerates the 43%-vs-39% gap).
2. **Unnecessary decorative elements** (e.g., a background flag image behind the bars) that add "chart-junk" without adding information, and can bias perception of the data (this connects to the data-ink-ratio principle above).
3. A simple two-bar-height illustration in the slides (bars "A" and "B" at values 120 and 150) shows the same principle abstractly: whether the y-axis starts at 0 or is truncated close to the bar heights changes the *visual impression* of how different A and B are, even though the underlying numbers haven't changed.

**General lesson:** a graph can be **factually/numerically accurate** and still be **designed to mislead** through axis manipulation, added visual "noise," or selective framing — so both the maker and reader of a chart need to check these features (axis start point, added imagery, aspect ratio) before trusting a visual impression of "how big" a difference is.

### How to choose the right plot
The lecture organizes plot selection around **"What would you like to show?"** — four broad goals, each branching to specific chart types:

| Goal | Sub-cases | Plot types |
|---|---|---|
| **Comparison** | among items; over time (few vs. many periods/categories) | Bar charts (horizontal/vertical), variable-width charts, line charts, circular/radial area charts |
| **Relationship** | two variables; three variables | **Scatter plot** (two variables); **Scatter plot with bubble size** (three variables — the third variable mapped to point size) |
| **Distribution** | single variable, few vs. many data points; two variables | **Bar histogram** (few points), **Line histogram** (many points), **Scatter plot** (two variables) |
| **Composition** | changing over time vs. static; relative-only vs. relative-and-absolute differences | **Stacked bar/area charts**, **100% stacked charts**, **pie charts**, **waterfall charts**, **treemaps** |

Beyond this decision tree, the lecture lists further organizing questions to ask when choosing a plot:
- Are you showing **distributions & compositions**, **proportions**, **data distributions**, **comparisons/group differences**, **associations/relationships between variables**, **geographical data**, or is the choice constrained by **variable type** (as in §32/§33's tables)?

### Category-specific plot notes from the slides

- **Proportions (part-to-whole composition):** pie charts, stacked/stream area charts, 100% stacked bar charts, and **area/treemap plots** and **waffle/pictogram charts** ("1 square equals 1%") for showing market-share-style compositions.
- **Data distribution (indicative of potential groups or group differences):** histograms and boxplots, e.g., a bimodal histogram of "response time" data or side-by-side boxplots for groups A/B/C.
- **Group differences:**
  - **Main-effects and interaction plots** (line plots with condition on the x-axis and separate lines per group, e.g., "Low anxiety" vs. "High anxiety" across "Computer-assisted / Lecture / Discussion" instruction types) — crossing lines visually indicate an **interaction effect**.
  - **Violin plots** — combine a boxplot's summary statistics (median, IQR) with a **mirrored, smoothed density curve** showing the full shape of the distribution (wider = higher probability density at that value), e.g., "Chick weights by feed type."
  - **Raincloud plots** — combine a half-violin (density), a boxplot, and the **raw individual data points** ("rain") all in one plot, for maximum transparency about both summary statistics and the underlying raw distribution (e.g., the "Overlap between 6 months and 1 year listening history" example, split by personality trait).
- **Association between variables:**
  - **Scatter plots** — for two numeric (X, Y) variables, to observe their relationship.
  - **Bubble plots** — scatter plots where **point size** encodes a third variable (and sometimes **color** encodes a fourth, categorical-or-continuous variable), e.g., GDP per capita (x) vs. life expectancy (y) vs. population (bubble size) vs. birth rate (color).
- **Temporal (showing change over time):** line charts, bar charts by time period, and **streamgraphs** (flowing, stacked area charts that show both a **category's individual trajectory** and the **overall total** over time simultaneously, e.g., "Twitter Topic Stream for Top Users").

### Initial-data vs. final-results visualization — the not-exhaustive list
The lecture explicitly presents a set of plot types loosely organized along an "initial exploration" ↔ "final result/communication" spectrum, while noting this list is **not exhaustive** and that **some plots are used for both purposes**:
- Histograms, Boxplot, Scatter plot, Violin plot, Raindrop (raincloud) plot, Mosaic plot, Spider plot / Radar chart, Circos plot, Radial heat map, Streamgraph, Pie charts & bar charts, Funnel plots.

## 37. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 12)

- **Descriptive statistics:** statistics that organize, summarize, and describe gathered/collected data (as opposed to generalizing beyond it).
- **Inferential statistics:** statistics used to make generalizations from a sample to a population and to test hypotheses.
- **Central tendency:** the most typical/representative value of a set of data; measured by mean, median, or mode.
- **Mean:** the arithmetic average of a set of values.
- **Median:** the middle value of an ordered data set.
- **Mode:** the most frequently occurring value in a data set.
- **Dispersion (spread):** the degree to which values in a data set vary around the central/typical value.
- **Range:** the difference between the maximum and minimum values in a data set.
- **Interquartile Range (IQR):** the range spanning from the 25th percentile (Q1) to the 75th percentile (Q3); the "middle 50%" of the data.
- **Variance (σ²):** the average of the squared deviations of each value from the mean.
- **Standard Deviation (SD):** the square root of the variance; a measure of spread in the same units as the original data.
- **Normal distribution:** a symmetric, bell-shaped distribution where mean = median = mode, and ~68%/95%/99.7% of data fall within 1/2/3 SDs of the mean.
- **Z-score:** a standardized score, Z = (x − μ)/σ, expressing how many SDs a value is from the mean; allows probability calculation and cross-distribution comparison.
- **Skewness:** asymmetry in a distribution's shape.
- **Negative (left) skew:** long tail toward lower values; Mean < Median < Mode; often from a ceiling effect on easy tasks.
- **Positive (right) skew:** long tail toward higher values; Mode < Median < Mean; often from a floor effect (e.g., reaction time).
- **Ceiling effect:** many scores cluster near the maximum possible value, compressing the ability to detect further improvement/variation.
- **Floor effect:** many scores cluster near the minimum possible value, compressing the ability to detect further decrease/variation.
- **Bimodal distribution:** a distribution with two distinct peaks, often indicating two underlying subgroups.
- **Boxplot:** a plot showing minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum, and outliers (beyond 1.5×IQR from the box edges); useful for diagnosing distribution shape and outliers at a glance.
- **Data-ink ratio:** a visualization design principle — maximize the proportion of "ink" (visual elements) devoted to actual data, minimizing decorative/non-informative elements ("remove to improve").
- **Misleading (but accurate) graph:** a chart that is numerically correct but designed (e.g., via truncated axes, added imagery) in a way that visually distorts the reader's impression of the data.
- **Violin plot:** a plot combining boxplot summary statistics with a mirrored density curve showing the distribution's shape.
- **Raincloud plot:** a plot combining a half-violin density curve, a boxplot, and raw jittered data points.
- **Bubble plot:** a scatter plot where point size (and sometimes color) encodes additional variable(s) beyond the x/y axes.
- **Streamgraph:** a stacked, flowing area chart showing both individual category trajectories and an overall total over time.
- **Interaction plot:** a line plot (typically with condition on the x-axis, separate lines per group) used to visualize main effects and interactions between two or more factors; crossing lines suggest an interaction effect.

## 38. Validity — Lecture 13

The lecture opens by distinguishing **Validity** and **Reliability** from a cluster of related-but-different concepts: **Repeatability, Reproducibility, Replicability,** and **Generalisability** (defined in §40 below). A useful mental model presented visually (target/phenomenon vs. measured variable):
- **Valid & reliable:** measurements cluster tightly around the true value of the actual phenomenon.
- **Reliable but not valid:** measurements cluster tightly together, but consistently *away* from the true value (consistent, but consistently wrong).
- **Valid but not reliable:** measurements are scattered/inconsistent, but their *average* is centered on the true value.

The lecture poses this as an open question for behavioral research specifically: *"can/should we expect [perfect valid+reliable measurement] in behavioural research?"* — flagging that human behavior is inherently noisier and harder to pin down than, say, a physical measurement, which is why practices like **Open Science (sharing data & methods)**, **larger sample sizes**, and **pre-registration** are highlighted as tools the field uses to compensate.

### Definition
**Validity** = the extent to which a test, tool, or study **accurately measures what it claims to measure**. More fully: the **appropriateness, correctness, meaningfulness, and usefulness** of the specific inferences researchers make based on the data they collect and analyse.

**High validity** = results correspond to real properties, characteristics, and variations in the physical or social world.

### Broad divisions of validity (four types, from the lecture's overview diagram)
1. **Construct validity** — the degree to which the measured variable actually truly measures the conceptual variable (the *construct*) it is designed to assess.
2. **Internal validity** — confidence that you can make **causal statements** about your study, resulting from ruling out (or properly handling) **bias, confounding, and random errors**.
3. **External validity** — generalizable to other contexts (e.g., other datasets, other people).
4. **Statistical conclusion validity** — the degree to which conclusions about the relationship among variables, based on the data, are correct or "reasonable" (i.e., the outcomes are not simply due to chance).

### Construct Validity
**Definition:** the degree to which the measured variable actually measures the conceptual variable (the construct) it is designed to assess. The research/test should be **well-constructed**, using established standards and methods.

**Worked "valid or not?" examples from the slides:**

| Proposed operationalization                      | Generally considered:                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Happiness Index = Job Satisfaction               | Questionable - too narrow; happiness is broader than job satisfaction alone                                            |
| IQ = GRE                                         | Questionable - GRE measures specific academic-test skills, not the full construct of "intelligence"                    |
| Acute Stress = Stress Questionnaire              | Reasonable, if a well-validated questionnaire is used                                                                  |
| Acute Stress = Results of a task requiring focus | Questionable - a focus task is a downstream *effect* of stress, not a direct or comprehensive measure of the construct |
| Addiction = Standard Addiction Questionnaire     | Reasonable, if standardized/validated                                                                                  |
| Depression = Standard Depression Questionnaire   | Reasonable, if standardized/validated                                                                                  |
| Depression = Implicit Association Test (Bias)    | Questionable - an IAT measures implicit bias/associations, which is a different construct than clinical depression     |

**Threats to construct validity:**
- **Narrow construct definition** — e.g., defining "Happiness Index" as merely *Salary + Job Satisfaction*, which leaves out large parts of what "happiness" actually means.
- **Narrow level of construct** — e.g., only testing one drug dosage level, when the construct ("drug effect") really spans a dose-response range.
- **Bias (intentional or unintentional) in design** — e.g., an IQ test containing very specific questions that rely on knowledge that isn't actually common/general (so it partly measures prior education/culture rather than pure "intelligence").
- **Bias in explaining the construct (leading)** — e.g., asking "what emotion does this ad *portray*?" (leading the participant toward a specific expected answer) vs. the more construct-faithful "what emotion do *you* feel while looking at this ad?"

### Internal Validity
**Definition:** established when research demonstrates a genuine **causal relationship** between two variables — i.e., "is the observed effect genuine? Did your manipulation of X really affect Y?" It is the result of ruling out (or properly handling) **bias, confounding, and random errors**, and is indicative of good construct validity in the study.

**Threats to internal validity:**
- **Placebo effects** — e.g., in a music therapy trial, participants feel better simply because they *believe* they're receiving an effective intervention, not because of the music itself.
- **Selection bias** — e.g., studying the effect of a new math-teaching app by recruiting students from a top private school to use the app, and comparing their scores to students from an average public school who *don't* use the app: the groups differ in many other ways (socioeconomic background, teacher quality, resources, prior achievement) besides just app usage, so any score difference is confounded.
- **Not accounting for confounding variables** in general.
- **Demand characteristics (hypothesis guessing)** — knowing (or guessing) what the experimenter is testing may lead participants to change how they respond. E.g., a participant who thinks "the researcher wants to see if sad music makes me sad" may adjust their responses to fit that expectation, rather than responding "naturally."

**Worked "good internal validity" example:** a study where mice lost weight when they exercised on a wheel is judged to have good internal validity because it (a) used good experimental practices like random samples, (b) accounted for control variables that might independently cause weight loss (diet change, disease, age, etc.), and (c) accounted for confounding variables that might affect the data.

### External Validity
**Definition:** can the results be generalized...
- ...beyond the people who participated? → **population validity**
- ...to other contexts, settings, and real-life circumstances? → **ecological validity**

**Threats to external validity:**
- **Sampling bias** — e.g., studying only psychology undergraduates' reactions to music and claiming the results apply to all age groups (directly echoes the **WEIRD** sampling critique from §26).
- **Ecological validity / artificial lab conditions** — e.g., memory research that asks participants to recall word lists in a quiet lab cubicle, versus real-world memory (remembering a shopping list in a noisy supermarket with distractions); or decision-making research using hypothetical monetary gambles on a computer, versus real-life financial decisions that involve emotions, stakes, and social factors not captured in the lab.
- **Cultural differences** — e.g., a survey on attitudes toward authority developed in Australia and used in China *without adaptation*; the concept of "authority" may be understood differently across cultures, so results may not mean the same thing in both contexts.
- **Temporal validity (time period effects)** — e.g., surveying daily commuting stress on crowded public transport in large cities: are pre-COVID results still valid now?

**Mitigating threats to external validity:**
- *Sampling bias* → recruit diverse samples; use **stratified random sampling** (§26).
- *Ecological validity* → conduct **field studies**; design tasks closer to real-world activities.
- *Temporal validity* → replicate studies over time; update constructs/instruments for new contexts.
- *Cultural differences* → **cross-cultural validation**.

**The Hawthorne Effect** is presented as a special case bridging internal and external validity: it refers to the **generalizability of behavior under observation** — e.g., workers in a factory improve productivity simply *because they know* researchers are observing them. The lecture poses this explicitly as a discussion question: is the Hawthorne Effect a threat to external validity, internal validity, or both? (Argument for **internal**: the "true" cause of the productivity change is the awareness of being observed, not the actual intervention being tested — a confound. Argument for **external**: behavior observed in the study setting may not generalize to real-world/unobserved settings, since people behave differently when they know they're being watched.)

A related discussion example posed in the lecture: *"Does a noisy crowd influence the points judges award in a boxing match?"* — framed as a case to think through in terms of both internal and external validity.

### Statistical Conclusion Validity
**Definition:** inappropriate statistical procedures, or other statistical errors, may be responsible for the *appearance* of a difference or correlation that **does not represent reality**.

Key requirements to protect statistical conclusion validity:
- **Adequate sample size** (ties to §26's discussion of effect size, power, and sample size).
- **Right statistical tests** (choosing a test appropriate to the data's scale of measurement and distribution — ties to §32–§34).
- **Accounting for errors** (e.g., Type I/Type II error awareness — ties to §17 and §26).

### Worked examples table — "what's the main validity problem?"

| #   | Research question                         | Study design (brief)                                                                                                                                                                                                                 | Main validity problem                                                                                                                                     |
| --- | ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Does music improve exam performance?      | Researcher plays classical music to students while they study, compares exam scores to a control group who studied in silence — but the music group studied in a quiet library while the control group studied in a noisy classroom. | **Internal validity** — the difference could be due to the study environment (quiet library vs. noisy classroom), not the music.                          |
| 2   | Are teenagers addicted to social media?   | Researcher surveys 50 students from one private school and concludes teenagers today are addicted to social media.                                                                                                                   | **External validity** — the sample is too narrow to generalize to all teenagers.                                                                          |
| 3   | Does coffee make people more intelligent? | Participants drink coffee, then complete a difficult reasoning test; the coffee group performs better, and the researcher concludes coffee increases intelligence.                                                                   | **Construct validity** — a single reasoning test does not necessarily measure "intelligence" as a whole construct.                                        |
| 4   | Does a mindfulness app reduce stress?     | Participants use a mindfulness app for two weeks and report reduced stress; researcher concludes the app caused the reduction. There is no control group or pre-test.                                                                | **Internal validity** — the stress reduction could be due to exams ending, time passing, placebo effects, etc., not the app.                              |
| 5   | Are people happier after exercising?      | Researcher asks people leaving a gym "How happy do you feel right now?" — most report being happy, so the researcher concludes exercise makes people happier.                                                                        | **Selection bias + external validity** — people who choose to exercise (and who agree to answer) may already differ systematically from people who don't. |

## 39. Reliability — Lecture 13

### Definition
**Reliability** = the **consistency and stability** of a research instrument (e.g., a measure or score). **Any measure used in research should be reliable — otherwise it's useless.** Reliability is essentially the **repeatability of a method/test or of research findings**.

### Two broad "kinds" of reliability, by what's being assessed
1. **Reliability of tools/methods or the measuring device.**
2. **Reliability of people** (participants, or occasionally researchers/raters).

### Threats to reliability
- **Measurement error:** equipment malfunction, human error, or ambiguous wording in survey questions.
- **Instrumentation changes:** the measurement instrument itself is not consistent across repeated measurements — changes in the instrument can introduce variability and hurt reliability.
- **Practice effects:** participants may improve performance on a task purely due to practice/learning, producing different results on subsequent administrations (independent of any real underlying change).
- **Sampling variability:** in experiments with small sample sizes, random fluctuations in participant characteristics can lead to unreliable results.
- **Participant error:** any factor that adversely alters the way a participant responds/performs — e.g., interviewing someone at 11am vs. 6pm (fatigue, time-of-day effects).
- **Participant bias:** any factor producing a false/biased response — e.g., a non-anonymous mental health questionnaire, where participants may not answer honestly.
- **Researcher error:** any factor that alters the researcher's interpretation — e.g., fatigue effects from interviewing all day.
- **Researcher bias:** any factor inducing bias in the researcher's recording of responses — e.g., subjective interpretation that (perhaps unconsciously) skews recording toward the expected/desired "result."

### Reliability of tools/methods (four sub-types)
The lecture organizes tool/method reliability around two axes — **stability over time** and **coherence/equivalence across items or versions**:

1. **Stability (test-retest reliability):** does the measurement device produce **consistent results regardless of the time of measurement**? Does it produce the **same results when retested on the same people on a different occasion**?
2. **Internal consistency:** relevant when a scale/instrument uses **multiple items meant to measure the same construct** — evaluates whether these items *consistently measure the same underlying concept*. Is the device consistently measuring what you want it to measure?
   - **Cronbach's alpha** — typically used for questionnaires/surveys; computed from the correlation matrix among all items (worked example in the slides: a 5-item scale across 100 respondents, with item-pair correlations combined into an overall α, e.g., α = .78 for the full scale, with individual item-subset αs like .85, .79, .78, .82 shown as components).
   - **Split-half reliability:** uses only *some* of the available correlations — compare results on one half of the items to the other half. If the test is reliable, people's scores on each half should be similar.
3. **Parallel forms reliability:** obtained by administering **different versions** of an assessment tool (both versions must probe the same construct/skill/knowledge base) to the same group of individuals, and comparing results. This can **avoid some problems inherent to test-retest** (e.g., practice effects from seeing the *same* items twice).
4. **Coherence of attributes** constituting the method/tool/apparatus — the general idea (spanning stability and internal consistency together) that the various parts/items making up an instrument should hang together coherently.

**Worked example — personality inventories:** the **Myers–Briggs Type Indicator** (16 personality types) vs. the **NEO Personality Inventory** (5 personality dimensions/factors) are presented as a comparison point for discussing tool reliability (the NEO-PI/Big Five is generally regarded as having stronger psychometric — including reliability — support than the MBTI, though the slide itself simply juxtaposes them as a discussion prompt).

### Reliability of people (participants and researchers)
1. **Inter-rater / Inter-observer reliability:** the **degree of agreement between two (or more) participants or observers'** simultaneously recorded measurements.
   - Measured via **correlation**; also helps with **outlier detection** (a rater who disagrees sharply with the others may be flagged).
   - **Cohen's kappa** is the named statistic for this (used for categorical/nominal agreement between exactly two raters).
   - A live discussion question posed in the slides: **"How many annotators per dataset?"** — i.e., how many independent raters/coders are actually needed to get a trustworthy inter-rater reliability estimate (more raters generally → more robust kappa/agreement estimate, but at higher cost).
2. **Intra-observer reliability:** the **degree of agreement within the same observer's** measurements on **repeated occasions** (i.e., does the same rater agree with themselves over time?).
3. **Reliability of researchers:** conceptually similar to participant reliability, but **not common** in practice; can be assessed in **qualitative research** when there is more than one Principal Investigator (PI) — e.g., in **qualitative thematic analysis**, where multiple researchers independently code the same transcripts and their agreement is checked.

### Tests of reliability — summary table (from the slides' overview diagram)
| Reliability type | Associated statistical tests |
|---|---|
| **Intra-rater** | Kendall's coefficient of concordance, Cohen's kappa, Fleiss' kappa, Krippendorff's Alpha, Pearson's correlation |
| **Parallel (forms)** | Pearson's correlation |
| **Internal consistency** | Cronbach's Alpha, Split-Half, Kuder-Richardson-20/21 |

*(Note: the slide groups Cohen's kappa, Fleiss' kappa, Krippendorff's Alpha, and Kendall's coefficient of concordance together under a general "agreement across raters/occasions" umbrella in its diagram — these are in practice mostly used for **inter-rater** agreement, with Cohen's kappa specifically for two raters and Fleiss' kappa/Krippendorff's alpha for more than two; treat the diagram's placement as the lecture's own organizing scheme even where finer distinctions exist in the wider literature.)*

## 40. Repeatability, Reproducibility, Replicability, and Generalisability — Lecture 13

The lecture opens with these four terms placed alongside Validity and Reliability, signaling that they are related-but-distinct concepts worth keeping straight (the slides present them without extensive elaboration beyond this framing, but the intended distinctions — consistent with how these terms are used in the broader open-science/behavioral-research literature the course draws on — are):

- **Repeatability:** obtaining the same results when the **same researcher(s)** repeat the **same study**, using the **same methods, same equipment, and under the same conditions**.
- **Reproducibility:** obtaining the same results when a study is repeated using the **same data and methods**, but potentially by a **different researcher/team** (emphasis on independent verification of the analysis/computation itself).
- **Replicability:** obtaining the **same/consistent results** when the study is conducted **again from scratch** (new data collection), potentially by different researchers, under similar (but not necessarily identical) conditions — this is the concept most closely tied to the "replication crisis" in behavioral science.
- **Generalisability:** the extent to which findings hold **beyond** the exact conditions of the original study — overlaps substantially with **external validity** (§38).

These four concepts, together with **Open Science practices** (sharing data and methods openly), **larger sample sizes**, and **pre-registration** (publicly committing to hypotheses and analysis plans *before* collecting/analyzing data, which prevents post-hoc "p-hacking" or cherry-picking of results), are presented as the field's core toolkit for improving trust in behavioral research findings — directly addressing the lecture's opening question of whether we can/should expect perfectly valid-and-reliable measurement in behavioral research.

## 41. Additional Glossary Terms (Lecture 13)

- **Validity:** the extent to which a test, tool, or study accurately measures what it claims to measure; the appropriateness/correctness/meaningfulness/usefulness of the inferences drawn from data.
- **Construct validity:** the degree to which a measured variable truly measures the conceptual construct it is designed to assess.
- **Internal validity:** confidence that a study supports a genuine causal relationship, resulting from ruling out bias, confounding, and random error.
- **External validity:** the degree to which results generalize beyond the study's participants (population validity) and beyond the study's setting (ecological validity).
- **Statistical conclusion validity:** the degree to which conclusions about relationships among variables, based on the data and statistical procedures used, are correct (not simply due to chance or statistical error).
- **Population validity:** a component of external validity — can results generalize to people beyond those who participated?
- **Ecological validity:** a component of external validity — can results generalize to real-life contexts/settings beyond the (often artificial) study setting?
- **Placebo effect:** an internal-validity threat where participants show a change simply because they believe they are receiving an effective intervention.
- **Selection bias:** an internal-validity threat where comparison groups differ systematically on characteristics other than the manipulated variable, confounding the results.
- **Demand characteristics (hypothesis guessing):** an internal-validity threat where participants alter their behavior/responses based on cues about what the experimenter is hoping to find.
- **Cultural differences (as a validity threat):** an external-validity threat where a construct or instrument developed in one cultural context is applied, without adaptation, in a different cultural context where it may not mean the same thing.
- **Temporal validity:** an external-validity threat/consideration — whether findings still hold across different time periods.
- **Hawthorne Effect:** the tendency for people to change their behavior because they know they are being observed; a threat spanning both internal and external validity.
- **Reliability:** the consistency and stability of a research instrument or finding; repeatability of a method/test.
- **Test-retest reliability:** a form of tool reliability — whether a measurement device gives consistent results when the same people are retested on a different occasion.
- **Internal consistency:** a form of tool reliability — whether multiple items on a scale consistently measure the same underlying construct.
- **Cronbach's alpha:** a statistic commonly used to assess internal consistency, especially for questionnaires/surveys.
- **Split-half reliability:** an internal-consistency method comparing scores on one half of a test's items to scores on the other half.
- **Parallel forms reliability:** reliability assessed by administering two different-but-equivalent versions of a test to the same group and comparing results.
- **Inter-rater (inter-observer) reliability:** the degree of agreement between two or more raters/observers recording the same measurements simultaneously; often assessed via Cohen's kappa (two raters) or Fleiss' kappa/Krippendorff's alpha (more than two raters).
- **Intra-observer reliability:** the degree of agreement within the same rater/observer's measurements across repeated occasions.
- **Cohen's kappa:** a statistic for inter-rater agreement between exactly two raters, typically for categorical data.
- **Kuder-Richardson 20/21 (KR-20/KR-21):** internal-consistency reliability statistics, typically used for tests with dichotomous (right/wrong) items.
- **Repeatability:** obtaining consistent results when the same researcher(s) repeat the same study under the same conditions.
- **Reproducibility:** obtaining consistent results when a study's data/methods are independently re-analyzed, potentially by a different researcher/team.
- **Replicability:** obtaining consistent results when a study is conducted again from scratch (new data collection), potentially under different conditions/by different researchers.
- **Generalisability:** the extent to which findings hold beyond the exact conditions of the original study; overlaps with external validity.
- **Open Science:** a set of practices (sharing data, sharing methods/materials, pre-registration) intended to improve the trustworthiness, transparency, and replicability of research findings.
- **Pre-registration:** publicly committing to a study's hypotheses and planned analyses before data collection/analysis begins, to prevent post-hoc result-fishing.
